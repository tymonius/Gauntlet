# Gauntlet Lore Development Notes

**Status:** Early setting-development notes; no complete canon has been approved.  
**Scope:** Setting, tone, faction-world relationship, and rejected lore directions. This file is not rules text.

---

## 1. Development approach

Build the setting slowly.

Do not attempt to write the full history, world map, creation myth, political system, or definitive campaign premise in one pass. Establish one preference at a time, test whether it feels right, and preserve uncertainty where no decision has been made.

Lore should support the game rather than explain every rule object.

---

## 2. Current tonal and material direction

The material culture should feel confidently **colonial / early American**, centered broadly on the mid-to-late eighteenth century, with room for:

- flintlock muskets, bayonets, rifles, cannon, cavalry, dragoons, officers' sabers, regulars, militia, minutemen, artillery crews, and frontier companies;
- clapboard towns, brick civic buildings, meetinghouses, wharves, warehouses, taverns, counting houses, mills, stockades, blockhouses, earthworks, roads, bridges, ships, and river craft;
- white-marble or pale-stone neoclassical buildings with columns, domes, porticos, and broad stairs when secular political authority must be communicated architecturally;
- separate austere Inquisition architecture built from dark brick, rough stone, heavy timber, limewash, narrow windows, enclosed courtyards, tribunal halls, fortified chapels, archives, and secluded compounds;
- printing presses, parchment, wax seals, ledgers, maps, dispatches, field equipment, laboratory tools, ritual objects, and early-modern institutions;
- early laboratories, workshops, water-powered mills, foundries, shipyards, manufactories, surveying instruments, optics, clocks, and physical communications;
- old structures, strange places, unreliable testimony, and partially understood supernatural powers as background texture.

Move away from pikes, armor, medieval infantry, castles as the default seat of power, and any visual midpoint between knights and eighteenth-century soldiers.

This is a visual, technological, and institutional reference point, not a requirement to recreate United States history. The setting does not need arbitrary visual deviations merely to prove that it is fictional. Avoid the literal American flag and direct United States national symbols; otherwise, colonial and early American architecture and design may be used plainly.

Mystery and supernatural danger are welcome, but should not become the entire premise. Gauntlet is primarily a conflict game about institutions, maneuver, rivalry, and power.

Working tonal principle:

> The world should feel materially grounded, politically contested, and only partially understood, while the immediate story remains centered on faction conflict.

The [Illustration Art Direction](Gauntlet_Illustration_Art_Direction.md) governs the production-facing visual interpretation of this setting.

## 3. Factions as institutions

The six factions represent **institutions and methods of power**, not six fixed nations, ethnic groups, uniformed orders, or isolated cultures.

Current factions:

- Military;
- Diplomats;
- Inquisition;
- Mystics;
- Financiers;
- Intelligence.

This allows a deck or player force to represent a coalition, court, army, order, guild, expedition, office, conspiracy, or institutional bloc rather than a nation-state.

The factions may arise within a broadly shared civilization or political sphere while drawing members from different cultures, regions, professions, and social classes. They can share laws, ranks, currencies, technologies, religious language, and political traditions while competing over how power should be exercised.

Faction membership does not imply shared dress or ethnicity. The Ranger and Spymaster, Alchemist and Spirit Walker, or Grand Inquisitor and Witch Hunter may look radically different while remaining mechanically and institutionally linked.

---

## 4. Current working premise

A promising, but not yet final, direction is:

> The six institutions once served or participated in a larger overarching order. That central authority collapsed, fractured, disappeared, or lost legitimacy. The institutions continued operating without a trusted center and now compete to preserve, restore, redefine, or dominate the shared civilization.

Possible versions of that collapse include:

- a dead or vanished Crown;
- a fractured ruling council;
- a fallen, sealed, occupied, or inaccessible capital;
- a failed sacred, legal, or magical mandate;
- a combination such as a Broken Council following the fall of the capital.

No version has been selected yet.

The institutions should not necessarily think of themselves as villains or rebels. Each may claim that it alone can preserve order, prevent catastrophe, or restore legitimate authority.

## 5. The Gauntlet in the setting

The Gauntlet should remain primarily **the game's metaphor and abstraction**, not a defined geographical feature.

To run the Gauntlet is to overcome the opponent's full sequence of resistance, force them back, and prevail in the final Last Stand battle. The term does not require an in-world mountain pass, ancient road, campaign corridor, chain of strongpoints, or named region called the Gauntlet.

A match may abstract many kinds of struggle:

- a military campaign;
- a fight for a city, fort, port, bridge, or road network;
- competing political control;
- institutional suppression or subversion;
- an economic takeover;
- a covert operation;
- a ritual or doctrinal contest;
- a combination of several methods of power.

Individual Territories may depict roads, high ground, towns, depots, bridges, monasteries, arenas, and other strategic locations without implying that the six cards form a literal recurring route in the setting.

The positions beyond the Territory column are rules endpoints, not named regions. A **Last Stand** is the name of the battle created after a player runs the Territory column, not a place that characters enter or pursue one another into.

Do not over-explain why every conflict uses the same game structure. The board is an abstraction of the contest, not a map that lore must reproduce literally.

## 6. Supernatural and religious direction

The supernatural is real, but its public interpretation remains contested.

Mystic practices may arise from very different traditions. The Alchemist approaches hidden forces through experiment, matter, and natural philosophy; the Spirit Walker practices an inherited native ritual tradition. Their shared faction reflects supernatural power, not a common culture or costume.

The Inquisition is zealously anti-magic and actively hunts magic users. Its officials may privately recognize that supernatural events are real while publicly denying, suppressing, or redefining them according to doctrine. Witch Hunters operating in the field are especially likely to encounter supernatural forces directly while remaining committed to their destruction.

The Inquisition's religion should be a fictional dominant tradition rather than a direct representation of a real-world religion. Its broad intellectual atmosphere may resemble severe revivalist or Puritan thought, including the belief that an earlier age of miracles has ended and the present age requires judgment and punishment for wickedness. Do not add detailed doctrine, rites, symbols, or denominational equivalents without separate approval.

Supernatural phenomena should usually enrich political and institutional conflict rather than replace it.

## 7. Rejected or deprecated directions

Do not revive these as the default premise without an explicit new decision.

### Direct Old World / New World analogue

Rejected:

- fantasy Europe discovering a fantasy America;
- explicit colonial powers arriving in an inhabited New World;
- a historical-colonial geopolitical narrative mapped closely onto real-world conquest.

Colonial and early American architecture, clothing, warfare, and material design remain the approved visual foundation without requiring that geopolitical structure.

### Literal geographical Gauntlet

Rejected or superseded:

- the Gauntlet as one universal strategic corridor;
- the Gauntlet as a specific road, pass, causeway, valley, river route, or chain of strongpoints;
- card-shaped terrain becoming literal world geography;
- the Last Stand as a location.

### Age of Claims / colonial legal fiction

Rejected as too explanatory and politically overbuilt:

- formal claim systems as the central reason Gauntlets exist;
- sanctioned colonial wars deciding legal title;
- excessive emphasis on legitimacy, charters, and territorial claims as the world premise.

Those ideas may appear locally where appropriate, especially for Diplomats or Financiers, but should not define the whole setting.

### Ancient-road cosmology

Rejected or set aside:

- ancient roads reconnecting distant realms or capitals;
- the Gauntlet as a sacred succession mechanism;
- a universal metaphysical explanation for every battlefield;
- one vanished empire explaining all ruins, magic, politics, and conflict.

### Universal legendary prize

Do not assume every faction is racing toward the same throne, vault, weapon, garden, god, or relic. Such an object may work in a specific scenario, but not as the mandatory setting premise.

### Mystery as the whole identity

Do not turn Gauntlet into primarily an expedition, treasure-hunt, lost-city, or exploration game. Mystery and strange terrain should enrich the conflict rather than replace it.

### Forced visual unfamiliarity

Do not insert unusual architecture, invented natural features, unfamiliar construction, or arbitrary decorative oddities merely to announce that the setting is not real-world America. Fictionality should emerge from Gauntlet's own institutions, characters, conflicts, and supernatural undercurrent.

## 8. Relationship to art direction

The [Illustration Art Direction](Gauntlet_Illustration_Art_Direction.md) establishes the shared production standard for:

- colonial and early American architecture and interiors;
- neoclassical secular authority;
- separate Inquisition architecture;
- landscape and terrain;
- profession-first character and clothing design;
- flintlock military technology and logistics;
- restrained supernatural depiction;
- faction identity through action rather than costume color.

The [Leader Design Bible](Gauntlet_v0.6_Leader_Design_Bible.md) governs the specific approved design of each individual Leader.

Lore should explain coexistence and rivalry without forcing every faction into a separate culture, national identity, or visual uniform.

## 9. Open setting questions

Develop one at a time:

1. What exactly collapsed: Crown, Council, Capital, Mandate, or a combination?
2. How long ago did the collapse occur?
3. Are the factions formally at war, operating through proxies, or competing inside a nominally shared government?
4. What geographic and political scale does a normal match abstract?
5. How common is the phrase "run the Gauntlet" within the world, if it is used in-world at all?
6. How public and accepted are Mystic practices?
7. What is the Inquisition religion's relationship to civil government and ordinary community life?
8. How independent are chartered financial and intelligence institutions from military and civil authority?
9. What non-faction populations, local powers, towns, and communities inhabit the world?
10. How much supernatural certainty should the setting provide to players and characters?

Do not answer all ten before the core premise has been tested through smaller discussions.
