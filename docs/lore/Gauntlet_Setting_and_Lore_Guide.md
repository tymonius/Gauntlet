# Gauntlet Setting & Lore Guide

**Status:** Working canon, version 0.224  
**Last consolidated:** August 2026  
**Scope:** Setting baseline, institutional factions, material culture, visual-lore guardrails, and representative unnamed settler polities

**Revision 0.224:** Raised the setting's supernatural baseline from rare enough to remain broadly deniable to **uncommon but established in aggregate, institutionally consequential, and capable of affecting history**. Most people still do not encounter unmistakable magic in ordinary life, reliable practitioners remain rare, and supernatural effects resist routine reproduction or industrial use. Governments, armies, churches, scholars, financiers, diplomats, and intelligence networks nevertheless possess enough accumulated experience that they cannot safely treat all supernatural claims as mere superstition. Clarified that **Arcane is a cross-faction category rather than a synonym for Mystics allegiance**. Mystics remain the principal specialists in deliberate supernatural practice, but other institutions may employ, investigate, regulate, exploit, or suppress isolated Arcane methods. Reframed the Inquisition's hostility toward magic: it need not deny anomalous phenomena, but rejects “magic” as a neutral or legitimate category of practice and contests what such phenomena are, who may manipulate them, and who has authority to judge their use. Established that rare supernatural events may have lasting political, military, religious, commercial, legal, and diplomatic consequences and may provide setting-native points of divergence from familiar history without turning Gauntlet into a high-magic world.

**Revision 0.223:** Established broad social and cultural identities for the six major settler polities, including the stereotypes they commonly apply to one another. The old river province is proud, hospitable, status-conscious, landed, politically ambitious, and protective of personal independence; the maritime commonwealth is practical, cosmopolitan, argumentative, commercially minded, tolerant, and fiercely municipal; the religious settlement is disciplined, literate, communal, morally serious, and highly conscious of public reputation; the chartered territory is orderly, contractual, professionally competent, commercially mobile, and accustomed to institutional hierarchy; the frontier province is self-reliant, improvisational, suspicious of distant authority, neighborly, and accustomed to danger; and the autonomous city is urbane, ceremonious, cosmopolitan, intensely civic-minded, and proud of its antiquity and privileges. Their corresponding negative stereotypes emphasize aristocratic expansionism, evasive commercialism, self-righteous intrusion, calculating company dependence, lawless unreliability, and decadent attachment to faded imperial prestige. These are broad regional tendencies and mutual stereotypes rather than universal traits of individual residents.

**Revision 0.222:** Reserved the emblem of the future united nation formed by the six major settler polities: a single solid, regular six-pointed star in the colonial or heraldic mullet tradition, with one point representing each founding polity. It is not a hexagram and is not constructed from two interlocking triangles. The game's G-over-star mark is product branding rather than the in-world national emblem.

**Revision 0.221:** Established the relative military and naval capacities of the six major settler polities without imposing a single overall power ranking.

**Revision 0.220:** Established the relative population, urban hierarchy, and settlement patterns of the six major settler polities without fixing exact census figures.

**Revision 0.219:** Established the broad regional economic identities of the six major settler polities without fixing every crop, workshop, mine, harbor, or trade route.

## 1. How to Use This Guide

This guide distinguishes among three kinds of material:

- **Confirmed canon:** Decisions already accepted and safe to build upon.
- **Deliberately unresolved:** Questions intentionally left open for later development.
- **Superseded or rejected:** Earlier directions that should not reappear without an explicit decision to reconsider them.

New lore should be developed incrementally. Establish one place, institution, practice, or conflict at a time rather than attempting to write a complete world history in a single pass.

### Present-Day Timeline Boundary

The guide's active present is fixed **shortly after the recent continental war and during the earliest postwar revenue, enforcement, quartering, and constitutional disputes**. Resistance remains predominantly local, loyalist, reformist, and constitutional. Inter-polity political coordination is tentative, irregular, and built upon older ad hoc congress practices rather than a mature continental movement.

Current development should fill gaps within that present: the six polities, native nations, institutions, factions, faiths, economies, laws, settlements, social life, material culture, geography, recent history, and supernatural realities already operating at this moment. It should not continue advancing the political sequence one crisis at a time.

Later deadly confrontations, permanent resistance organizations, mature continental congresses, rebellion, political union, independence, war against the parent realms, and any resulting constitutional order belong to a **future expansion or later-era setting guide**. Their broad possibility may be acknowledged where necessary, but their specific events, leaders, chronology, and outcomes should remain deliberately undeveloped until that later project.

### Reserved Future-Era Emblem

Although later political union and independence remain outside the active present-day development queue, one broad future-era outcome is reserved: the six major settler polities eventually separate from their parent realms and unite as one nation. The principal civic emblem of that nation is a **single solid, regular six-pointed star**, shaped in the colonial or heraldic mullet tradition rather than as a hexagram or two interlocking triangles.

Each point represents one of the six founding polities, while the complete star represents their union into one political whole. The emblem belongs to the future nation independently of the game's title or branding. A stylized **G** placed over the star may be used as the product logo for *Gauntlet*, but it is not the future nation's in-world emblem. The correspondence between six star points and the game's six institutional factions is an out-of-world design resonance, not the official in-world origin of the symbol.

The future nation's name, constitutional order, route to independence, exact flag and seal designs, colors, adoption history, official language surrounding the emblem, and later political uses remain deliberately unresolved for a future expansion or later-era setting guide.

# PART I — CORE SETTING

## 2. The Setting in One Paragraph

Gauntlet takes place in a vibrant, pre-industrial, powder-and-parchment society broadly comparable in material culture to the eighteenth-century colonial Atlantic world. Settler provinces, chartered territories, autonomous cities, commercial possessions, and independent native nations form a shifting political patchwork. Distant parent realms retain nominal authority and overseas connections, but local institutions have matured over several generations and largely govern themselves. Six institutional spheres—Military, Diplomats, Inquisition, Mystics, Intelligence, and Financiers—perform necessary social functions while pursuing overlapping interests through different methods. **The supernatural is real and uncommon, but sufficiently documented and consequential that governments, churches, scholars, armies, financiers, diplomats, and intelligence networks cannot safely ignore it. Reliable practitioners remain rare, supernatural effects resist routine reproduction, and their causes and limits remain fiercely disputed.**

## 3. What “Gauntlet” Means

The Gauntlet is **not a literal place**. It is not a road, pass, mountain range, fortified corridor, territory, institution, or named geopolitical region.

In-world, to **run the gauntlet** means to pursue an objective through the full resistance of an opposing power.

A match is an abstract representation of two sides struggling or competing through escalating resistance. It may represent a military campaign, political confrontation, commercial contest, covert struggle, religious dispute, or some mixture of these. It does not require a fixed geographic route or literal timescale.

The players’ faction leaders and decks characterize the methods, relationships, assets, and institutional strengths available to each side. They do not necessarily represent sovereign nations or formal alliances composed exclusively of one faction.

## 4. Tonal Baseline

The setting should feel:

- vibrant, active, and socially dense;
- politically layered and institutionally contested;
- materially grounded in the late gunpowder, pre-industrial world;
- colorful and alive rather than uniformly grim or drab;
- mysterious without becoming arbitrary;
- dangerous primarily for human, environmental, and political reasons;
- supernatural phenomena remain uncommon in ordinary life but can carry disproportionate personal, institutional, and historical consequences.

Gauntlet should feel **more fantastical than straightforward alternate history without becoming high fantasy**. Supernatural power should appear often enough in history, rumor, law, war, scholarship, and institutional practice to shape the world, while remaining unreliable and exceptional enough that ordinary society still depends on muskets, ships, roads, printing, medicine, credit, diplomacy, and human labor rather than magical infrastructure.

The world is not high medieval fantasy, industrial steampunk, Gothic ruin fantasy, or an alternate retelling of specific real-world religions and nations.

## 5. Historical and Technological Level

The material baseline is roughly eighteenth century.

Common technology includes:

- smoothbore muskets, rifles, pistols, bayonets, swords, and artillery;
- sailing warships, privateers, armed merchant vessels, ferries, barges, and river craft;
- horses, wagons, stagecoaches, post roads, ferries, canals, and bridges;
- printing presses, newspapers, pamphlets, broadsides, and extensive correspondence;
- handcraft production, mills, workshops, foundries, shipyards, and early manufactories.

The setting is firmly **pre-rail, pre-steam, and pre-industrial**. It should not drift toward Victorian technology, modern bureaucracy, or steampunk aesthetics.

Likewise, avoid medieval military shorthand: no castle-dominated political order, plate-armored field armies, pike-dominated warfare, or inherited ancient fortifications.

## 6. Geography

The continent should feel broadly compatible with eighteenth-century eastern North American geography without reproducing a one-to-one map. Its settled and contested regions may include:

- long coastlines and excellent natural harbors;
- broad navigable rivers and tidal estuaries;
- fertile coastal plains and river valleys;
- forested hills and mountain chains;
- marshes, wetlands, islands, and difficult interior terrain;
- meaningful climatic variation from north to south.

The continent is **not empty wilderness**. Native nations know, inhabit, govern, travel through, farm, hunt, trade across, and politically contest the landscape. Settler maps may be incomplete, contradictory, propagandistic, or legally self-serving because knowledge and jurisdiction are contested—not because geography routinely changes through magic.

Exact coastlines, borders, river systems, mountain ranges, distances, and climatic zones remain unresolved until a map is developed.

## 7. Population and Settlement

Population density is low by modern standards but the world should not feel empty. Major ports are crowded and cosmopolitan; market towns, county seats, religious communities, forts, company settlements, native towns, ferries, mills, roads, and river traffic produce concentrated social density.

Among the six major settler polities:

- the **old river province** is clearly the most populous, with the largest principal city, broadest agricultural settlement, and greatest raw demographic and militia weight;
- the **religious settlement** is second in population and has the most compact, continuous, readily mobilized town-and-farm settlement pattern around a major port;
- the **maritime commonwealth** is somewhat smaller but unusually urban, commercially concentrated, and distributed among several closely connected ports and coastal towns;
- the **chartered territory** has a moderate population concentrated along company ports, river settlements, forts, warehouse towns, and commercial corridors;
- the **frontier province** is smaller but fastest-growing, widely scattered, difficult to count, and expanding around forts, crossings, valleys, and disputed grants;
- the **autonomous city** governs the smallest total population but concentrates a substantial urban population inside a compact jurisdiction.

The likely urban hierarchy places the old river province's principal city first, the religious settlement's principal port second, and the autonomous city and largest maritime-commonwealth port among the next tier, followed by the chartered territory's company capital and the frontier province's comparatively modest administrative seat.

Exact populations, growth rates, census methods, treatment of transient and frontier residents, and city sizes remain unresolved.

## 8. Historical Depth

The present settler societies are old enough to have deep local identities, inherited institutions, family networks, property disputes, and political memories. Settler presence has developed over roughly **a century and a half to two centuries**, varying by region.

This is not a world built atop visibly ancient empires or fantasy ruins. The oldest settler buildings are old **for the current civilization**, not remnants of a lost age. Native nations possess their own much older histories, landscapes, political memories, and sacred places, but they should not be reduced to archaeological remnants or mysterious precursors.

## 9. Parent Realms

Three major overseas parent realms divide nominal authority over the six principal settler polities in a **3–2–1 pattern**.

### The Dominant Parent Realm

The dominant realm claims the old river province, maritime commonwealth, and religious settlement. It is a maritime, commercial, mixed-constitutional power broadly comparable in function to eighteenth-century Britain without being a literal fantasy Britain.

Its government combines:

- a hereditary crown with real executive authority, especially in foreign affairs, war, appointments, charters, and colonial administration;
- a bicameral legislature, with a representative lower chamber and an upper chamber of hereditary grandees, senior clergy, and other privileged figures;
- ministers chosen under the crown but increasingly dependent on legislative support;
- common-law courts and an inherited constitutional tradition;
- powerful banks, merchants, insurers, creditors, landowners, and chartered companies.

The lower chamber's control of taxation and appropriations makes sustained policy dependent on bargaining among crown, ministers, legislators, landed interests, and commercial interests.

The present colonial dispute is constitutional rather than simply anti-monarchical: the parent government claims broad authority to legislate for and tax its possessions, while settlers increasingly argue that internal taxation and local law require meaningful consent through their own elected institutions. Both sides understand themselves as defending inherited constitutional principle.

The dominant realm has an established or officially favored branch of the shared settler faith, closely connected to state institutions but substantially less doctrinally extreme than the Inquisition.

### The Principal Rival Realm

The principal rival realm claims the chartered territory and frontier province. It is more centralized in military and diplomatic affairs than the dominant realm and has relied heavily on forts, commercial corridors, negotiated native alliances, licensed traders, and company structures to maintain continental power with a smaller settler population.

Its relationship with the inland confederacy is especially important: in formal diplomacy the confederacy is treated as a sovereign ally whose obligations arise from negotiated agreement, while official maps and administrative language may ambiguously imply protection, dependency, or inclusion in the realm's sphere. The confederacy rejects unilateral descriptions as a source of jurisdiction.

The recent continental war ended in a clear but limited defeat for this realm. It lost strategic ground and influence but retained both of its principal settler polities and remains a major continental power.

### The Third Parent Realm

A diminished third realm retains the autonomous city as its principal major settler possession. Its continental position is weaker than that of the other two parent realms, but its old legal claims, cultural prestige, commercial ties, and surviving political relationships remain important.

Its history includes a former **crown-backed religious tribunal and reign of terror** that is distinct from the present Inquisition faction. That historical institution was abolished or radically reformed long ago; its archives, victims, surviving memory, and political use remain unresolved. It should not be conflated with the current Inquisition's stronghold polity or organizational structures.

### The Recent Continental War

The recent continental war involved the dominant realm, principal rival realm, their settler possessions, and multiple native nations pursuing their own interests. It should serve a structural role comparable to the French and Indian War without reproducing it one-for-one.

The war was fought not merely overseas but for the defense and control of continental settlements, forts, waterways, trade routes, and native relationships. The dominant realm achieved a clear but limited victory. The principal rival retained its two settler polities. Native nations experienced sharply different wartime and postwar outcomes.

The war left the dominant realm deeply concerned with debt, defense costs, western settlement, customs enforcement, and the expense of troops kept on the continent. Its response includes new or expanded revenue measures, professional enforcement, naval patrols, customs officers, broad search powers, admiralty proceedings, permanent garrisons, and quartering requirements. Quartering troops in private homes occurs often enough to become a recognizable grievance.

One especially unpopular revenue measure is withdrawn, reduced, or suspended after opposition, but the realm explicitly preserves its underlying claim to legislate for and tax the polities. This mixed concession-and-enforcement response strengthens moderates who believe constitutional pressure can work while convincing more radical critics that the deeper issue remains unresolved.

The parent realm also restricts settlement beyond a postwar western line, both to reduce renewed conflict with native powers and to control land policy. That restriction becomes a major grievance for settlers, land companies, speculators, and provincial governments with western claims.

# PART III — POLITIES, CLAIMS, AND NATIVE POWERS

## 10. Political Patchwork

No single state controls the continent. Political authority is divided among:

- settler provinces and commonwealths;
- chartered company territories;
- autonomous cities;
- smaller commercial possessions and dependencies;
- frontier districts and disputed jurisdictions;
- independent native nations and confederacies;
- overlapping parent-realm claims.

The six major settler polities are:

1. the **old river province**;
2. the **maritime commercial commonwealth**;
3. the **religious settlement / Inquisition stronghold**;
4. the **chartered company territory**;
5. the **frontier province**;
6. the **autonomous city**.

The dominant parent realm claims the first three, the principal rival claims the next two, and the diminished third realm claims the autonomous city. Exact names and borders remain unresolved.

These six are the major settler polities relevant to the future union, not the only jurisdictions on the continent. Smaller towns, dependencies, islands, company enclaves, border districts, native nations, and disputed territories may exist around and between them.

## 11. Relations Among Settler Polities

The settler polities are culturally related but politically separate. They share enough language, legal tradition, religion, print culture, commerce, migration, and material life to recognize one another as part of a wider settler world, yet regional identities remain strong.

Disputes may involve:

- borders and overlapping grants;
- rivers and navigation;
- tariffs, customs, and embargoes;
- debts and commercial privileges;
- land companies and western settlement;
- extradition and jurisdiction;
- religious establishment and toleration;
- militia obligations and defense costs;
- smuggling and maritime enforcement.

There is **no permanent continental government**. Coordination takes place through correspondence, envoys, bilateral agreements, military conferences, trade negotiations, and temporary congresses.

### The Ad Hoc Congress Tradition

The dominant realm's three polities possess an older tradition of temporary congresses convened during shared military, diplomatic, commercial, or constitutional crises. A typical congress uses:

- equal instructed delegations;
- one vote per polity;
- rotating or neutral hosting;
- unanimity for common measures;
- ratification or implementation by home governments;
- no independent power to tax, legislate, conscript, or maintain a permanent executive or military command.

The chartered territory, frontier province, and autonomous city remain outside this three-polity congress but may participate through invited envoys, commercial agents, border councils, military conferences, observation, mediation, or occasional broader continental conferences.

At the present moment this is **not yet a revolutionary congress**. It is an existing coordination mechanism beginning to acquire new significance as postwar constitutional disputes overlap.

### Leadership Within the Dominant-Realm Bloc

No one polity permanently leads the three.

- The old river province has the greatest demographic, landed, militia, and representative-political weight.
- The maritime commonwealth possesses disproportionate commercial, financial, logistical, and institution-building power.
- The religious settlement exercises unusual ideological, moral, educational, and mobilizing influence.

Leadership shifts by issue. The old river province and maritime commonwealth are close economic partners and competing political leaders. The old river province and religious settlement are reliable constitutional partners and competing ideological leaders. Their disagreements matter precisely because neither simply follows the other.

Movement of people among settler jurisdictions is generally possible, but goods are heavily regulated and political membership remains local. Naturalization, voting rights, civil rights, and officeholding can differ by polity. Membership in a native nation is a separate political status governed by that nation's law and treaties.

## 12. Native Nations

Native nations are **full sovereign political and cultural societies**, not wilderness populations, primitive remnants, or a single pan-native culture. Their histories long predate settler arrival and include trade, migration, intermarriage, adoption, rivalry, raids, wars, military alliances, tributary and dependent relationships, mediation, negotiated borders, prisoner exchange, peace settlements, and changing coalitions.

Three representative political models are established as anchors for later development. Their real-world comparisons are development shorthand, not literal in-world identities.

### Coastal Decentralized People

Broadly inspired by Wampanoag political structure, the coastal people consist of multiple communities and local leaders connected through kinship, trade, shared cultural practices, diplomacy, and situational cooperation rather than a strong permanent central government.

Their political decentralization means different communities can make different wartime and diplomatic choices. During the recent continental war they were generally sympathetic to or aligned with the dominant realm, but participation varied by community and local interest.

Their relationship with the riverine paramountcy is longstanding but comparatively loose, shaped by trade, intermarriage, migration, diplomacy, and occasional earlier competition rather than permanent alliance or present hostility. Their relationship with the inland confederacy is more distant but still diplomatic and commercial. Some communities increasingly value confederate support, information, trade access, or mediation as settler pressure grows.

### Riverine Paramountcy

Broadly inspired by Powhatan-style paramountcy, this polity combines substantial central authority with constituent peoples and local leaders whose obligations must be maintained through reciprocity, persuasion, kinship, tribute, diplomacy, and negotiated authority.

The paramountcy remains formidable but is under greater pressure than the inland confederacy. Settler expansion, competing trade relationships, and the ability of local leaders to make direct agreements create internal tension between central coordination and local autonomy.

During the recent continental war the paramount leadership pursued **armed neutrality**, seeking to preserve the polity while constituent peoples and local communities made divided and locally motivated choices. Some cooperated with one side, some with the other, and some attempted neutrality.

The paramountcy and inland confederacy are former serious rivals. Earlier wars ended in a durable negotiated peace that regularized boundaries, trade, prisoner return, and diplomatic obligations without subordinating either power. They now maintain active trade and diplomacy and are moving toward cautious strategic cooperation in response to settler expansion, but they have no permanent alliance, mutual-defense guarantee, or shared government.

#### Present Pressure: Old River Province and Riverine Paramountcy

The most immediate postwar dispute between the old river province and the paramountcy concerns an **upper-river wartime corridor**. During the recent war, the paramountcy permitted dominant-realm and provincial forces to use specified sites and routes for defense—temporary forts, depots, roads, ferries, river landings, camps, observation posts, defined passage, and limited cultivation or timber cutting necessary to sustain garrisons.

The paramountcy understood these arrangements as temporary, purpose-specific wartime accommodations that did not transfer surrounding land, extinguish native jurisdiction, or authorize permanent civilian settlement. Provincial officials, land companies, garrison commanders, discharged soldiers, traders, and settler families increasingly argue that military investment, continued occupation, improvements, expense, sacrifice, and settlement created durable rights.

People have gathered around some posts. Surveys extend beyond originally permitted areas. Provincial courts recognize grants that paramountcy councils never approved. Confrontations remain frequent but below the level of general war: surveyors may be expelled or detained, cabins and fences dismantled, livestock turned away, militia patrols confronted, and both sides accuse the other of unlawful force and theft.

The dominant parent realm has imposed an interim policy: a limited number of strategically necessary garrisons may remain temporarily, but new civilian settlement, surveys, land grants, roads, cultivation, and expansion beyond existing occupied sites are frozen while an imperial commission reviews the original permissions, military needs, provincial claims, and paramountcy objections. Neither side accepts this as a legitimate final settlement.

### Inland Confederacy

Broadly inspired by the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, the inland confederacy is a durable union of member nations governed through a Grand Council and established diplomatic forms. It is not a unitary state; member nations retain meaningful autonomy while participating in a sophisticated shared framework for foreign relations and common concerns.

The confederacy is the strongest and most secure of the three representative native political models. During the recent continental war it became the **principal rival realm's most important sovereign native partner**, judging the dominant realm's expanding settler population, roads, forts, land companies, and western claims the greater long-term threat.

The alliance was strategic, not submissive. The confederacy never accepted rival-realm sovereignty over its homelands. Its members did not participate uniformly, and internal debates over war, neutrality, trade, and risk persisted.

The postwar relationship with the principal rival remains necessary but strained. The peace settlement was negotiated chiefly around imperial interests, leaving disputes over promises, restitution, evacuated or retained positions, neutralized areas, trade, and sovereignty. The rival realm describes the confederacy inconsistently: as a sovereign ally in treaties and councils, but through ambiguous language of protection, dependency, friendship, or sphere on maps and in administrative documents.

The confederacy's postwar relationship with the dominant realm is a wary formal peace: diplomacy and limited trade have reopened, but military readiness remains high and disagreements over sovereignty, boundaries, jurisdiction, and enforcement continue.

Its relationship with the frontier province is especially complex. Both were aligned with the principal rival during the recent war and continue frequent councils and trade, yet they are also territorial competitors. Provincial settlements, speculative grants, unauthorized expansion, and competing jurisdiction remain chronic sources of friction.

The confederacy's relationship with the chartered territory is a longstanding, sophisticated, treaty-bound commercial partnership. Company routes, warehouses, licensed traders, transport, and credit provide material advantages, while both sides distrust the other's efforts to convert commercial relationships into wider political claims.

### Shared Foundations and Differences

Native political cultures are built on specific kinship systems, adoption practices, diplomatic protocols, territorial norms, and ceremonial traditions that should be developed separately for each nation. No universal template should be imposed.

Warfare may include raids, war parties, fortifications, firearms acquired through trade, ambushes, alliances, captive-taking, adoption, prisoner exchange, and large coalition campaigns when necessary. Native warfare should not be romanticized as either uniformly small-scale or inherently less political than settler warfare.

Spiritual traditions are culturally specific. Ceremonial specialists, healers, diviners, dream interpreters, keepers of ritual knowledge, and other roles may exist under different names and rules. **Spirit Walker** is a settler umbrella term, not a universal native title.

Formal diplomacy is old and sophisticated. Councils, gift exchange, kinship metaphors, reciprocal obligations, interpreters, emissaries, treaty memory, and public ceremony matter. Settlers often misunderstand native political authority when they assume that one leader can permanently alienate land or bind every constituent community through a single agreement.

There is no permanent pan-native government. Wider councils or coalitions arise for particular purposes while each people remains sovereign.

## 13. Language

Most settler polities share a common language or closely related speech, with recognizable regional accents, vocabulary, and styles of expression. Parent-realm elites and immigrant communities may preserve additional languages.

Native nations possess distinct languages. Interpreters, multilingual traders, Rangers, diplomats, missionaries, captives, adoptees, and border families are therefore politically important. Misinterpretation can be accidental, strategic, or legally consequential.

No universal common tongue should erase linguistic difference merely for convenience.

# PART IV — THE SIX FACTIONS AS INSTITUTIONAL SPHERES

## 14. What the Factions Are

The six factions are **institutional spheres and methods of power**, not six nations, ethnicities, religions, or global monolithic organizations.

A polity may contain officers, diplomats, inquisitors, occult practitioners, spies, merchants, creditors, and company agents simultaneously. Individuals may cooperate across faction lines, hold overlapping roles, or oppose members of their own broad institutional sphere.

Leader titles are archetypal roles rather than unique offices necessarily held by one person across the entire world.

## 15. Military

Military represents organized force, command, logistics, fortification, occupation, readiness, and the conversion of battlefield success into political fact.

- **General:** the heroic field commander—mobile, inspirational, campaign-focused, and visually anchored by command from a **white horse**.
- **Commandant:** the disciplinarian and institutional military administrator—associated with drill, garrisons, forts, inspections, logistics, readiness, punishment, and holding ground.

The General leads through momentum and battlefield authority. The Commandant leads through structure, preparation, and discipline.

Military power may belong to parent-realm regulars, provincial forces, city militias, company troops, native allies, mercenaries, privateers, Rangers, or other organized armed bodies. The faction does not imply one permanent army.

## 16. Diplomats

Diplomats represent negotiation, legislation, recognition, treaties, public legitimacy, concessions, procedure, and the manipulation of political relationships.

- **Ambassador:** external-facing negotiation, correspondence, treaties, gifts, influence, backchannels, and relationships among governments and political communities.
- **Senator:** internal political authority—assemblies, statutes, procedure, debate, coalition, public legitimacy, and the power to authorize, obstruct, ratify, or condemn.

Diplomatic power does not imply benevolence. A treaty can be coercive; recognition can exclude; procedure can obstruct; concessions can conceal advantage.

## 17. Inquisition

The Inquisition is a strict, transregional current within the wider shared settler faith. It presents itself as guardian of authentic doctrine, moral order, and protection against spiritual corruption, forbidden practice, heresy, fraud, and supernatural danger. Many sincere members of the broader faith reject its methods, jurisdiction, or claims to exclusive doctrinal authority.

### The Stronghold Polity

One major settler polity is the Inquisition's principal stronghold. Its founding pattern is broadly Plymouth-like: a covenanting religious settlement whose severe religious institutions became deeply embedded in civil society over generations. It has matured into a populous port-and-town polity with commerce, manufacturing, printing, education, fisheries, shipbuilding, and sophisticated political institutions rather than remaining an isolated theocratic village.

The civil government and Inquisition remain institutionally distinguishable. The polity is not ruled directly by a single cleric, but strict orthodoxy significantly affects political standing, freemanship, office, education, censorship, public morality, and community reputation.

Two institutional centers coexist: civil government and the Inquisition's religious hierarchy. Their cooperation is close but not perfect; jurisdictional conflict remains possible.

### Grand Inquisitor

The Grand Inquisitor represents institutional religious authority: tribunals, doctrine, warrants, judgment, discipline, records, censorship, and formal coercion. The office is not simply the civil governorship. Appointment belongs to senior institutional processes rather than ordinary popular election.

### Witch Hunter

Witch Hunters are commissioned field investigators and pursuers. They travel, gather testimony, inspect alleged sites and objects, expose fraud, hunt forbidden practitioners, and respond to dangers that tribunals cannot address from a fixed seat. In the stronghold their authority may be overt and legally recognized; elsewhere it may depend on local cooperation or clandestine operation.

### The Inquisition's Contradiction

The Inquisition does not need to deny that anomalous or supernatural phenomena occur. Witch Hunters, tribunals, and senior inquisitors have accumulated too much direct evidence for simple denial to remain credible within the institution.

Instead, the Inquisition rejects **magic as a neutral or legitimate category of human practice**. It disputes what supernatural phenomena actually are, whether practitioners understand the forces they invoke, whether deliberate manipulation of those forces is morally or spiritually permissible, and who possesses legitimate authority to investigate or regulate them. Different inquisitors may interpret particular phenomena as spiritual deception, corruption, illicit communion, dangerous manipulation of creation, fraud mixed with genuine anomaly, or some combination of these explanations without the setting confirming which interpretation is correct.

Internally, field practice requires practical knowledge. Inquisitorial tribunals and Witch Hunters maintain case records, tests, containment methods, protective procedures, artifact-handling rules, accumulated warnings, and countermeasures derived from prior encounters. The institution may therefore publicly condemn Arcane practice while privately studying it closely enough to suppress it.

This contradiction should remain productive rather than neatly resolved. The Inquisition may be doctrinally hostile, politically intrusive, and frequently wrong about particular accusations while still possessing genuine expertise concerning some supernatural dangers.

## 18. Mystics

The Mystics encompass traditions and practitioners who deliberately engage with genuine supernatural phenomena. They do not possess a monopoly on the supernatural; rather, they are the institutional sphere most deeply concerned with understanding, invoking, negotiating with, transforming, or systematically exploiting it.

The supernatural is:

- real;
- uncommon in ordinary daily life but established in aggregate;
- irregular, difficult to reproduce, and resistant to standardization;
- capable of producing genuine and sometimes dramatic effects;
- sufficiently consequential that major institutions cannot safely ignore it;
- interpreted differently by competing religious, scholarly, cultural, and practical traditions;
- dangerous or unreliable enough to resist normalization as ordinary technology or infrastructure;
- capable, on rare occasions, of altering battles, political decisions, settlements, laws, reputations, commercial calculations, or wider historical events.

Most people encounter the supernatural indirectly through stories, inherited cautions, religious teaching, local incidents, disputed objects, institutional reports, or the consequences of events they did not witness. Many people may live their entire lives without personally observing an effect they regard as incontrovertibly supernatural.

Skepticism therefore remains normal, but it is usually skepticism about **particular claims, explanations, practitioners, or the category of “magic” itself**, rather than a requirement that an informed person deny every anomalous event. An educated skeptic may accept that poorly understood phenomena occur while rejecting spirits, alchemical theory, Inquisitorial theology, or any claim that those phenomena form one coherent supernatural system.

It should never become routine spellcasting performed openly on every street, nor should supernatural practice replace ordinary medicine, communication, transportation, warfare, industry, or administration.

### Arcane Practice Beyond the Mystics

**Arcane is a cross-faction category, not Mystics allegiance.** Mystics contain the greatest concentration of deliberate supernatural practice, but other institutions sometimes adopt isolated Arcane techniques when the potential advantage outweighs uncertainty, scandal, danger, or doctrinal objection.

Examples include:

- military forces retaining or tolerating a **War Witch** for exceptional campaigns;
- diplomats consulting **Diplomatic Divination** before negotiations or political decisions;
- financiers experimenting with **Actuarial Alchemy** in attempts to price, predict, hedge, or exploit anomalous risk;
- intelligence networks employing **Spectral Surveillance** or related methods when ordinary observation is insufficient.

Such practices need not be common, officially acknowledged, or uniformly trusted. A government may publicly distance itself from a practitioner it quietly employs. A financier may treat an alchemical result as one uncertain input among conventional calculations. A military officer may resent reliance on a War Witch while refusing to surrender an advantage that appears to work. An intelligence service may preserve reports of successful spectral methods while regarding most alleged practitioners as frauds.

This diffusion helps explain the Inquisition's broader hostility toward Arcane practice. From the Inquisition's perspective, the danger is not confined to an isolated Mystic fringe: supernatural methods can spread into respectable institutions precisely because they sometimes produce useful results.

### Supernatural Events and History

Major supernatural events are rare, but rare does not mean historically irrelevant. A single genuine anomaly may alter a siege, discredit an official, close a road, inspire a law, destroy or save a settlement, change the interpretation of a treaty, redirect investment, provoke an inquisitorial campaign, or become the unresolved center of a later political dispute.

When developing Gauntlet-specific history, supernatural involvement should therefore remain available as one cause among many. It should not be inserted into every event, and it should rarely replace political, economic, military, environmental, or human explanations altogether. Its strongest use is as an additional force capable of pushing otherwise familiar historical dynamics onto paths unique to Gauntlet.

Accounts of such events should often disagree. A military report may describe disease or sabotage where an inquisitorial record alleges forbidden practice; a native tradition may understand the same event through obligations or forces the settler account does not recognize; an Alchemist may later propose an entirely different mechanism. The setting need not adjudicate among them unless a particular story or game element requires a firmer answer.

### Reliability Ceiling

Supernatural practice must remain too irregular and costly to become dependable infrastructure. The setting should not normalize:

- armies routinely fielding battle mages;
- commercially standardized enchanted weapons;
- healing magic replacing medicine;
- supernatural communication replacing couriers and printed correspondence;
- magical transportation replacing roads, horses, ships, or wagons;
- industrialized alchemical production of reliable supernatural effects;
- supernatural creatures functioning as ordinary wildlife;
- ordinary citizens treating unmistakable magic as mundane.

The desired equilibrium is **rare but consequential magic**: important enough to shape institutions and history, unreliable enough that the eighteenth-century material world remains intact.

### Alchemist

The Alchemist is an educated experimental scholar operating at the boundary between natural philosophy, medicine, chemistry, occult tradition, fraud, and genuine supernatural practice. The archetype should feel like an early experimental scholar rather than a generic fantasy wizard.

Alchemists may emerge from universities, learned societies, private patronage, military research, medicine, mining, metallurgy, or commercial experimentation. Their work relies on laboratories, manuscripts, glassware, furnaces, vessels, balances, diagrams, instruments, strange materials, exact procedures, and repeated attempts to turn unstable phenomena into reproducible knowledge.

The Alchemist's worldview tends toward proceduralization: if an effect occurred, then the conditions should be isolatable, repeatable, improved, priced, weaponized, healed through, or transformed into general knowledge. The problem is that genuine supernatural effects often resist exactly that program.

### Spirit Walker

**Spirit Walker is a settler umbrella term**, not the self-description of one universal native religious profession. Different native cultures possess their own legitimate ceremonial specialists, healers, diviners, dream interpreters, ritual leaders, keepers of inherited knowledge, and other practitioners whose names, rules, obligations, and political positions are culturally specific.

Settlers group some of these roles together because they perceive recurring capacities involving spirits, dreams, altered states, death, transformation, divination, ritual protection, place-bound obligations, or communication across boundaries ordinary people cannot cross.

Legitimate ceremonial practice must remain distinct from accusations of forbidden, predatory, or transgressive practice. Native communities themselves may draw strong moral boundaries around grave violation, illicit transformation, abuse of the dead, harmful imitation, predation, coercive spirit dealings, or other forbidden rites.

Some frightening settler stories—voice imitation, animal transformation, corpse use, grave violation, impossible pursuit, speaking with the dead—may be superstition, accusation, misunderstanding, deliberate fearmaking, or genuine supernatural events. The setting should preserve uncertainty in individual cases rather than equating native spirituality with malevolence.

The established Spirit Walker leader may stand close enough to dangerous boundaries to understand them deeply and remain unsettling even when acting as a protector. That does not mean legitimate ceremonial practice is itself merely a softer form of predation.

Visually, the established canonical Spirit Walker is a lean adult man with long dark hair, a large branching antler headdress, stark chalk-white body and face paint with dark red linear markings, minimal layered hide and cord garments, and an intense severe presence. His visual language may draw on ritual-performance aesthetics but must not collapse into a generic “tribal shaman,” literal Diné skinwalker, European druid, or pan-native stereotype.

### Shared Ritual Practice

Alchemist and Spirit Walker share the **Ritual** gameplay system because their practices overlap at the level of supernatural action without implying that they share one theology or metaphysics.

Alchemical ritual is independently developed but has also been transformed through appropriation, adaptation, partial understanding, translation, and reinterpretation of older Spirit Walker practices encountered through native traditions. Alchemists tend to strip procedure from inherited cultural obligation and describe it as universal technique. Spirit Walkers may regard that confidence as dangerous ignorance.

The relationship should allow genuine exchange, theft, misunderstanding, syncretism, and parallel discovery without reducing native practice to raw material for settler magic.

### Deliberately Unresolved Metaphysics

The guide does **not** establish one final explanation of magic. It remains unresolved whether:

- spirits are independent beings, psychological phenomena, manifestations of natural law, or something else;
- alchemical transformations and Spirit Walker rites interact with the same underlying reality;
- divination reveals fixed futures, probabilities, hidden information, or something stranger;
- the dead can truly return, or what “return” means in apparent cases of reembodiment or necromancy;
- religious, alchemical, and native explanations are competing descriptions of one phenomenon or partly distinct realities.

No universal cosmology should be invented merely to make every card fit one theory.

## 19. Intelligence

Intelligence represents information advantage, espionage, clandestine networks, assassination, sabotage, codes, intercepted correspondence, false identities, informants, surveillance, and the strategic manipulation of what opponents know.

- **Ranger:** physical-space intelligence—scouting, frontier mobility, irregular warfare, terrain knowledge, ambush, reconnaissance, and survival.
- **Spymaster:** social and institutional intelligence—agents, ciphers, dead drops, intercepted letters, surveillance, bribery, covert networks, and manipulation from within respectable society.

The Ranger disappears into terrain; the Spymaster disappears into institutions and social life.

Intelligence networks cross borders by necessity. They may serve governments, armies, companies, political movements, religious bodies, or private patrons.

## 20. Financiers

Financiers represent the conversion of wealth, credit, debt, logistics, contracts, monopoly, and institutional ownership into political power.

- **Banker:** credit, debt, liquidity, public finance, bonds, notes, collateral, insurance, and the ability to fund governments or make them dependent.
- **Executive:** chartered-company power, concessions, warehouses, shipping, production, infrastructure, monopoly, extraction, administration, and the conversion of commercial authority into quasi-governmental control.

The Banker controls abstraction—credit and obligation. The Executive controls systems—routes, workers, warehouses, contracts, land, and operational capacity.

Financiers should not be caricatured as merely greedy rich people. They occupy necessary positions in a world where armies, governments, trade, settlement, and infrastructure all depend on capital and credit.

## 21. Factions and Borders

Faction institutions cross political boundaries unevenly:

- **Military** and **Diplomats** are often tied most directly to governments;
- **Financiers** and **Mystics** can operate through networks extending across jurisdictions;
- **Inquisition** claims religious authority beyond ordinary borders, though recognition varies sharply;
- **Intelligence** serves patrons and states but depends on cross-border networks, informants, and access.

No faction is confined to one polity, ethnicity, social class, or parent realm.

# PART V — SOCIETY

## 22. Social Order

The setting is unequal and status-conscious, but comparatively fluid.

Local elites include:

- prosperous merchants;
- established landowners;
- officials and magistrates;
- officers;
- clergy;
- lawyers and physicians;
- university-trained scholars;
- prominent family networks.

Traditional hereditary nobility has little presence and is not socially dominant. Status is expressed through property, education, office, credit, family reputation, dress, patronage, and public conduct.

### Regional Social and Cultural Identities

The six major settler polities possess recognizable regional cultures shaped by their settlement patterns, economies, political institutions, religious habits, geography, and relationships with neighboring peoples. These descriptions identify broad tendencies and common stereotypes rather than fixed traits of every resident. Individuals, towns, classes, congregations, professions, and frontier districts may diverge sharply from the prevailing regional character.

- **Old river province:** Proud, hospitable, status-conscious, landed, politically ambitious, and strongly protective of personal independence. Public life places unusual weight on reputation, property, local office, hospitality, family standing, and the confidence of a mature province accustomed to thinking of itself as a major political actor. Outsiders commonly stereotype its residents as aristocratic, expansionist, overconfident, and inclined to treat provincial interests as continental interests.
- **Maritime commonwealth:** Practical, cosmopolitan, argumentative, commercially minded, tolerant, and fiercely municipal. Its ports and towns prize bargaining skill, local autonomy, maritime competence, religious latitude, and the ability to navigate overlapping laws and markets. Outsiders commonly stereotype commonwealth residents as evasive smugglers, slippery legalists, and merchants capable of turning every principle into a bargain.
- **Religious settlement:** Disciplined, literate, communal, morally serious, and highly conscious of public reputation. Its culture prizes education, civic participation, public argument, communal obligation, religious seriousness, and visible conformity to accepted standards of conduct even amid genuine political disagreement. Outsiders commonly stereotype its residents as austere, intrusive, censorious, and self-righteous.
- **Chartered territory:** Orderly, contractual, professionally competent, commercially mobile, and accustomed to institutional hierarchy. Company service, written agreements, regulated trade, administrative expertise, and the practical routines of maintaining a dispersed commercial network shape public expectations. Outsiders commonly stereotype its residents as calculating company dependents, overly comfortable with bureaucracy and monopoly, and uncertain in loyalty whenever corporate and civic interests diverge.
- **Frontier province:** Self-reliant, improvisational, suspicious of distant authority, generous toward neighbors, and accustomed to danger and uncertainty. Practical competence, local reputation, mutual aid, mobility, weapons familiarity, and the ability to solve problems without waiting for formal institutions carry unusual social weight. Outsiders commonly stereotype frontier residents as lawless, violent, provincial, unreliable, and unwilling to recognize obligations beyond their immediate communities.
- **Autonomous city:** Urbane, ceremonious, cosmopolitan, intensely civic-minded, and proud of its antiquity, charter, harbor, and inherited privileges. Residents identify strongly with the city itself and its status as an old, sophisticated, self-governing port connected to several commercial and cultural worlds. Outsiders commonly stereotype them as decadent, duplicitous, excessively formal, commercially opportunistic, and sentimentally attached to the faded prestige of the diminished parent realm.

## 23. Labor and Slavery

Chattel slavery is not part of the setting’s social or economic baseline.

This is not because coerced labor would be literally impossible or never profitable in a pre-industrial society. Rather, the world’s laws, settlement patterns, labor institutions, and political development never produced a large, hereditary, racialized plantation-slavery system. Nor does a later cotton-style technological and market revolution occur to make such a system overwhelmingly dominant.

Labor may include:

- independent family farming;
- tenant farming;
- wage labor;
- seasonal and migrant labor;
- apprenticeships;
- fixed-term contracted service;
- household service;
- debt and company dependency;
- penal labor in harsher jurisdictions.

Exploitation remains possible through debt, monopoly, rent, wages, contracts, land claims, company stores, and control of transportation or supplies. The setting need not be egalitarian merely because it excludes ownership of people.

## 24. Landholding

Independent family farms and small holdings are culturally important.

Larger estates, company concessions, and concentrated landownership also exist. Land claims may overlap, frontier surveys may conflict, and settler grants may trespass upon land governed under native systems.

Native nations retain their own forms of territorial authority and land use. These should not be reduced automatically to settler-style private property.

## 25. Gender Roles

Gender expectations broadly resemble those of the eighteenth century.

Formal politics, military command, senior clergy, higher education, and the learned professions are predominantly male. Women’s legal and political participation is restricted.

Women nevertheless exercise substantial influence through:

- households and family businesses;
- kinship networks;
- property and inheritance in particular circumstances;
- patronage and social reputation;
- religious and charitable communities;
- informal political communication;
- trade and skilled work.

Widows and unmarried women may possess greater legal and economic independence than married women. Female officeholders, scholars, military leaders, or other formal authorities can exist, but they are notable exceptions rather than evidence of a modern social order.

## 26. Household and Family

The household is the basic social and economic unit.

A household may include:

- immediate family;
- older or extended relatives;
- apprentices;
- hired workers;
- servants;
- lodgers;
- business partners or dependents.

Homes frequently double as workplaces. Farms, taverns, shops, workshops, and professional offices may all operate as family enterprises.

Marriage, inheritance, property, kinship, and family reputation strongly affect access to work, credit, education, office, and public influence.

# PART VI — ECONOMY, INFORMATION, AND INSTITUTIONS

## 27. General Economy

The economy is centered on ports, rivers, roads, and credit.

Cash is often scarce. There is no single universal currency. Transactions may involve:

- local and foreign coin;
- banknotes and company notes;
- letters of credit;
- bills of exchange;
- promissory notes;
- book credit;
- commodity exchange and barter.

Value depends on metal content, reputation, institutional trust, convertibility, distance, and the solvency of the issuer.

### Regional Trade and Specialization

The settler world contains strong regional economic specialization without requiring every polity to depend on a single staple. Geography, climate, soil, waterways, founding patterns, labor availability, access to overseas markets, and relationships with native nations shape what each region produces and trades.

Different polities or districts may become known for combinations of:

- tobacco, grain, livestock, orchard crops, fibers, and other agricultural staples;
- timber, pitch, tar, resin, and other naval stores;
- fishing, preserved foods, coastal commerce, and maritime services;
- shipbuilding, rope-making, sail-making, ironworking, milling, and other practical manufactures;
- mining, quarrying, ceramics, glass, paper, printing, and specialist crafts;
- warehousing, finance, insurance, brokerage, and long-distance trade;
- inland exchange conducted through river towns, frontier posts, native trade networks, and overland routes.

No major polity is entirely self-sufficient. Ports depend on agricultural interiors; inland producers depend on merchants, credit, transport, and overseas markets; manufacturing centers depend on imported raw materials and food; and frontier commerce depends heavily on relationships with neighboring native nations. This interdependence encourages regular trade while also creating vulnerability to embargoes, war, crop failure, transport disruption, monopoly, and financial panic.

The six major settler polities have broad economic identities:

- the **old river province** combines tobacco-led agriculture, grain, livestock, river commerce, landed wealth, and expanding local shipping;
- the **maritime commonwealth** specializes in overseas trade, shipbuilding, fishing, insurance, credit, printing, and small manufacturing;
- the **religious settlement** combines fisheries, timber, shipbuilding, metalworking, printing, and comparatively strong household and town production;
- the **chartered territory** centers on timber, naval stores, furs, inland transport, fortified warehouses, and company-controlled commercial corridors;
- the **frontier province** produces livestock, grain, timber, furs, land claims, and military supplies while remaining resource-rich, cash-poor, and dependent on outside credit and transport;
- the **autonomous city** functions as an entrepôt through warehousing, brokerage, ship repair, customs revenue, tolerated smuggling, nearby islands, and warmer-climate produce from compact dependencies.

Exact commodity mixes, manufacturing centers, extraction sites, and routes remain unresolved.

### Local Manufacturing and Imported Goods

Manufacturing remains pre-industrial and dispersed through households, farms, workshops, mills, yards, and small manufactories rather than mechanized factories. Most communities produce many ordinary necessities locally, while major ports and specialized districts support larger concentrations of skilled labor.

Common forms include:

- gristmills, sawmills, fulling mills, and other water-powered works;
- smithies, foundries, gunsmiths, wheelwrights, coopers, carpenters, and other metal or wood trades;
- shipyards, ropewalks, sail lofts, chandlers, and maritime repair trades;
- tanneries, breweries, distilleries, bakeries, potteries, glassworks, paper mills, and print shops;
- household spinning, weaving, sewing, food preservation, and other domestic production;
- small manufactories that gather more workers and equipment under one proprietor without becoming modern industrial factories.

Imported goods remain important, especially fine cloth, luxury furnishings, specialized tools, scientific instruments, medicines, books, fashionable finished goods, and products requiring skills or supply chains not available locally. Wealthier consumers display status partly through access to such imports.

Parent-realm trade policy may deliberately favor overseas manufacturers or restrict competing local production, making manufacturing another source of political friction. Wartime shortages, embargoes, boycotts, or patriotic campaigns can encourage local substitutes and new workshops, but the economy remains firmly pre-industrial.

## 28. Printing and Public Information

Printing is widespread and politically influential.

Common printed materials include:

- newspapers;
- broadsides;
- political pamphlets;
- sermons;
- advertisements;
- legal notices;
- commercial circulars;
- sensational accounts;
- serialized stories;
- caricatures and prints.

Reliable information travels slowly. Newspapers are partisan. Mail can be delayed, altered, intercepted, or selectively published. Rumor and personal correspondence remain powerful.

Prior restraint is uncommon in many jurisdictions, but printers and authors may still face prosecution, licensing pressure, libel actions, sedition charges, loss of patronage, denial of postal access, interference with supplies, or commercial retaliation after publication. The balance differs sharply by polity.

The press may eventually support a separate **Media** faction. A Yellow Journalist remains a possible future leader archetype. This remains reserved design space; propaganda and public-opinion mechanics should not be permanently absorbed into Diplomats or Intelligence before that decision is made.

## 29. Postal and Courier Networks

Communication travels through overlapping provincial, parent-realm, commercial, military, and private networks. Major settlements are connected by post riders, stage routes, packet boats, river craft, coastal shipping, and private couriers. Service is reasonably regular on principal corridors and much less dependable in frontier districts, during bad weather, or across hostile jurisdictions.

Official correspondence, commercial packets, newspapers, and private letters share the same networks but do not receive equal treatment. Government dispatches and paid express service may receive priority, while ordinary letters can wait for a carrier, vessel, or sufficiently full mailbag. Newspapers often circulate through favorable postal rates or institutional arrangements because printers and governments both value their distribution.

Security is imperfect. Mail may be delayed, lost, damaged, copied, intercepted, opened under legal authority, or secretly examined by intelligence networks. Codes, seals, trusted couriers, duplicate dispatches, and indirect routes are therefore common for sensitive correspondence. Control of routes, postal appointments, and access to the mails carries commercial and political power.

The result is a world in which communication is regular enough to connect distant polities, markets, newspapers, and political movements, but slow and uncertain enough that rumor, outdated instructions, and conflicting reports remain normal.

## 30. Education, Universities, and Learned Societies

Education is valued but unevenly available.

Settled regions may support:

- local schools;
- apprenticeships;
- academies;
- seminaries;
- private tutors;
- a small number of prestigious transregional universities.

Basic literacy is relatively common in settled communities, though not universal.

Universities are small, patronage-heavy, and early modern in organization. They educate clergy, lawyers, physicians, administrators, officers, scholars, and members of elite families rather than serving a mass student population. Formal instruction remains only one route into the learned professions: apprenticeship, private reading, clerking, and mentorship remain essential.

A small but influential network of academies, universities, and learned societies crosses political boundaries. Learned societies may include scholars, physicians, surveyors, engineers, officers, natural philosophers, collectors, and wealthy patrons. They exchange correspondence, publish proceedings, sponsor lectures and expeditions, collect specimens and maps, and recognize discoveries or useful improvements.

These intellectual networks often continue across provincial and parent-realm rivalries even when governments are in conflict. Access nevertheless remains shaped by wealth, patronage, social standing, books, and personal introductions.

Alchemists often begin within this respectable intellectual world, studying medicine, chemistry, natural philosophy, antiquities, languages, or related fields before moving into research that universities consider disreputable, forbidden, or dangerously speculative. Universities and learned societies can therefore function both as gateways into Alchemy and as institutions that publicly reject it.

## 31. Medicine and Supernatural Healing

Medicine is becoming more empirical and professional but remains incomplete and contested.

Practitioners may include:

- physicians;
- surgeons;
- apothecaries;
- midwives;
- traditional healers;
- military and naval medical practitioners.

Ordinary medicine remains the normal and necessary response to illness and injury. Cities and ports may also use quarantine, sanitation rules, temporary hospitals, and other practical public-health measures during epidemics.

Supernatural healing exists, but it is uncommon, unreliable, disputed in explanation, and never available enough to replace ordinary medicine.

- The dominant settler faith permits prayer, blessings, fasting, and appeals for divine mercy, but generally rejects the idea of a normal, repeatable profession of miracle-healing. Unexpected recoveries may be regarded as providential; practitioners who claim reliable supernatural cures attract suspicion.
- Spirit Walkers may combine inherited medicinal knowledge, plants, diagnosis, ceremony, and relationships with unseen forces. Outsiders often cannot distinguish where ordinary healing knowledge ends and genuine supernatural effect begins.
- Alchemists may pursue healing through experimental compounds, ritual procedures, transmutation, and attempts to manipulate hidden forces systematically. Such treatments can produce extraordinary results, but may be dangerous, inconsistent, expensive, forbidden, or poorly understood.
- Folk healers, charm-makers, revival preachers, and alleged miracle workers exist on the fringes. Some are frauds, some possess effective traditional knowledge, and a very small number may achieve results that cannot be explained conventionally.

The Inquisition is especially hostile to claims of reliable supernatural healing, interpreting them as fraud, heresy, forbidden practice, or evidence of dangerous powers.

The governing principle is: **supernatural healing may occasionally save someone ordinary medicine could not, but it is never safe, routine, universally accepted, or reliably available.**

### Popular Beliefs and Folk Practices

Ordinary people commonly observe charms, omens, protective customs, household remedies, weather signs, dream interpretations, funeral practices, lucky or unlucky associations, and other inherited folk traditions without considering themselves Mystics. Such practices may be learned through families, trades, rural communities, sailors, soldiers, immigrants, or cultural exchange with neighboring peoples.

Their status is deliberately ambiguous. Some are harmless custom, some preserve effective practical knowledge, some are fraud or coincidence, and a small number may touch genuine supernatural forces without their users fully understanding them. Educated skeptics may dismiss many such beliefs while privately observing a few familiar precautions of their own.

The Inquisition distinguishes—often inconsistently—between tolerated custom and forbidden superstition. It is more likely to intervene when a practice claims repeatable power, invokes unseen beings, uses recognizable ritual forms, challenges clerical authority, or becomes associated with unexplained harm. Most everyday folk practice remains below that threshold, though local inquisitors and magistrates may disagree sharply about where the line lies.

## 32. Crime, Vice, and the Informal Economy

Crime and unofficial commerce are common, visible, and often intertwined with legitimate trade, especially in major ports, contested border districts, and frontier settlements. The informal economy is not a wholly separate underworld; it runs alongside ordinary markets, shipping, taverns, warehouses, and public institutions.

Smuggling is widespread wherever tariffs, navigation laws, monopolies, embargoes, or inspection regimes make lawful trade expensive or politically unpopular. Merchants, ship captains, warehouse workers, customs officers, local officials, and respectable investors may participate directly or quietly tolerate it. Smuggling ranges from routine evasion accepted by much of a community to organized criminal enterprise.

Other recurring activities include:

- gambling, prizefighting, illicit taverns, and unlicensed entertainments;
- theft, burglary, highway robbery, piracy, and river banditry;
- fencing stolen goods through docks, markets, warehouses, and secondhand dealers;
- counterfeiting coins, notes, seals, contracts, and official documents;
- bribery, embezzlement, customs corruption, and abuse of patronage;
- organized dockside gangs and protection networks in the largest ports.

The boundary between crime and tolerated custom is often politically and socially contested. A smuggler may be praised locally as a trader resisting an unjust restriction while the same community condemns theft or violent robbery. Enforcement is selective and shaped by jurisdiction, reputation, wealth, political connections, local sympathy, and the interests of officials.

> **The informal economy is a shadow running alongside ordinary trade and public life, not a completely separate criminal world.**

## 33. Religion in Everyday Life

Most settler polities share variations of one broadly similar dominant faith. They recognize enough common doctrine, scripture, history, and religious language to regard one another as belonging to the same wider tradition, but local worship, church organization, preaching style, discipline, political influence, and tolerance may differ sharply. The faith is socially important but not universal, and ordinary religious life should never be reduced to the Inquisition.

Outside the Inquisition-dominated polity, the wider faith is **not governed by one perfectly unified church**. Different branches may retain ties to parent-realm churches, organize themselves locally, or coexist through looser arrangements. Formal religious establishment exists only in some jurisdictions. Where it does exist, an established church may be a local provincial branch, the parent realm's official church, or—in the Inquisition stronghold—the stricter inquisitorial expression embedded in government. Other polities may tolerate several branches or leave congregations largely self-governing.

Congregations, meetinghouses, preaching, charitable networks, scripture, family observance, public morality, schools, burial practices, fasts, thanksgivings, and local religious disputes shape community life. Some communities are comparatively broad or tolerant; others are austere, revivalist, or doctrinally strict.

The Inquisition represents an extreme and fundamentalist current within this shared faith rather than a separate religion. Its claim to defend authentic doctrine is disputed even by many sincere adherents, and its authority varies greatly by jurisdiction.

Missionary activity among native nations exists, but it should not be treated as a simple one-directional civilizing project. It can produce sincere conversion, translation, syncretism, accommodation, political alliance, resistance, family conflict, and diplomatic controversy. Native peoples retain their own religious traditions and authority structures; no single pattern applies across the continent.

The Inquisition is the most hostile current toward native ceremonial traditions, especially Spirit Walker practice. It is likely to describe such practices as fraudulent, superstitious, forbidden, or spiritually corrupt even when its own field agents know that real supernatural danger may be involved. Inquisitorial investigation or suppression can therefore become a diplomatic conflict with native governments that recognize Spirit Walkers as lawful ceremonial authorities.

The faith’s detailed cosmology, sacred history, scriptures, named denominations, and exact institutional structures remain deliberately underdeveloped.

# PART VII — LAW AND PUBLIC ORDER

## 34. Legal Order

Law is formal but fragmented. The settler provinces largely inherit a common-law tradition from their parent realms, comparable in function to the inheritance of English common law in early America. Judges rely on precedent, established procedure, legal commentary, and inherited principles, but the received law is continually modified by provincial statutes, municipal charters, local custom, commercial practice, court decisions, and treaty obligations.

The inherited common law therefore provides a shared legal language without producing a perfectly uniform system. A rule recognized in one province may be altered, limited, or rejected in another, and disputes often turn on which court or body of law has jurisdiction.

Depending on the jurisdiction, legal authority may include:

- magistrates;
- local courts;
- traveling provincial courts;
- juries;
- sheriffs and constables;
- municipal courts;
- company courts;
- military justice;
- Inquisition tribunals where recognized;
- native legal systems;
- treaty procedures.

There is no modern professional police institution.

## 35. Public Order

Cities rely upon:

- constables;
- magistrates;
- ward officers;
- a part-time night watch.

The watch patrols after dark, raises alarms, enforces curfews and fire regulations, and detains people caught in obvious offenses. Constables serve warrants, summon juries, move prisoners, and carry out magistrates’ orders.

Investigation is fragmented. Victims, witnesses, informants, newspapers, private rewards, interested officials, and factional networks may all drive a case.

Lawyers are an important learned profession rather than occasional courtroom specialists. They draft contracts and charters, advise merchants and landowners, argue civil and criminal cases, interpret overlapping jurisdictions, and often move between legal practice and political office. Commercial expansion, contested land claims, fragmented authority, and frequent charter disputes ensure a steady demand for legal expertise.

Most lawyers enter the profession by **reading law** under an established attorney. Training combines extensive independent study with practical clerking: copying pleadings, drafting contracts and petitions, observing court, assisting with cases, and learning how precedent and procedure operate in practice. Universities may offer lectures in law, rhetoric, political philosophy, and government, but they supplement rather than replace apprenticeship. Admission to practice is controlled by the courts and depends on examination, sponsorship, reputation, and demonstrated competence.

The profession is intellectually demanding but socially gated. Talented outsiders can enter, particularly in frontier districts where trained lawyers are scarce, but prominent families retain an advantage through education, books, leisure, introductions, and access to respected mentors.

A possible future **Legal** faction remains reserved design space. **Prosecutor** and **Chief Justice** are current leader-archetype possibilities, not confirmed faction leaders. Existing legal and procedural themes should not be permanently absorbed into Diplomats or the Inquisition before that faction decision is made.

### Case Reporting

Formal reporting of judicial decisions is emerging but incomplete. Major printers increasingly publish selected opinions and important cases, especially those involving charters, land, commerce, maritime law, or constitutional disputes. Much precedent still circulates through lawyers’ notebooks, manuscript copies, printed arguments, newspaper summaries, and professional correspondence.

Urban lawyers with strong libraries and correspondence networks therefore enjoy an important advantage. Practitioners in remote districts may rely more heavily on older commentaries, local custom, and incomplete reports. Disagreement over what a court actually decided is common.

### Judicial Independence

Senior judges are commonly appointed by governors or comparable charter authorities and hold long or indefinite tenure rather than standing for frequent election. This gives higher courts continuity and some independence from immediate electoral pressure.

That independence is incomplete. Judicial salaries and court budgets often depend on appropriations controlled by elected assemblies or other political bodies. Appointment, promotion, funding, and jurisdiction therefore remain entangled with politics. Exact arrangements differ by jurisdiction.

### Public Prosecution

Provinces and other major jurisdictions commonly appoint an **Attorney General** or comparable law officer to represent the government in major criminal prosecutions, charter disputes, revenue cases, and matters involving the public interest. Subordinate prosecutors or government advocates may be commissioned for particular courts, districts, or cases.

This is not yet a fully modern district-attorney system. Victims, complainants, magistrates, and private counsel may still initiate or help pursue lesser prosecutions, while serious felonies and offenses against government are increasingly treated as public matters.

### Punishment and Confinement

Criminal punishment generally emphasizes fines, restitution, bonds, public penalties, locally authorized corporal penalties, and comparatively short periods of confinement. Jails chiefly hold defendants awaiting trial, debtors, people awaiting sentence, and offenders serving short terms. The setting does not possess a mature modern penitentiary system built around long incarceration as the ordinary punishment.

The system is not lenient. Financial penalties can be ruinous, public and corporal punishments severe, and confinement unhealthy and degrading. Capital punishment remains lawful but is generally reserved for a comparatively narrow class of grave offenses such as murder, treason, piracy, and similarly serious crimes. Availability and frequency vary considerably by jurisdiction.

### Debt and Civil Enforcement

Creditors may enforce lawful debts through seizure or sale of property, attachment of commercial proceeds, claims against collateral, demands for sureties, and—in some jurisdictions—imprisonment of defaulting debtors. Negotiated extensions, composition agreements, refinancing, partial repayment, and arrangements with guarantors are common because creditors usually prefer recovery to destroying the debtor’s capacity to pay.

Insolvency is not automatically treated as fraud. Courts and merchants distinguish, however imperfectly, among honest failure, reckless borrowing, concealed assets, forged instruments, and deliberate evasion.

### Insolvency and Discharge

An honest insolvent debtor may petition the appropriate court, disclose debts and assets, surrender substantially all nonessential property, and seek release from confinement. Complete discharge of the unpaid balance is possible but exceptional rather than automatic.

There is no universal bankruptcy code. Different provinces, cities, company territories, and courts vary in their procedures, property exemptions, creditor-consent requirements, treatment of later-acquired property, and willingness to grant a true discharge. Fraud, concealment, false accounting, and transfers intended to defeat creditors bar relief and may lead to prosecution.

# PART VIII — WARFARE AND TRANSPORT

## 36. Armed Forces

Military forces may include:

- regular troops;
- provincial troops;
- militias;
- company forces;
- mercenaries and contract specialists;
- Rangers and irregular corps;
- naval personnel and privateers.

Smoothbore muskets and bayonets are standard for line infantry. Rifles are common among Rangers, hunters, marksmen, and irregular troops. Officers, cavalry, naval personnel, and close-combat troops may carry pistols and swords.

Artillery is central to fortification, sieges, coastal defense, and battlefield operations.

No polity dominates every military domain. The old river province possesses the greatest manpower and conventional land-war potential; the maritime commonwealth has the strongest locally controlled maritime capacity; the religious settlement maintains the most cohesive militia and organized coastal defense; the chartered territory has the strongest professional logistical and fortified-corridor apparatus; the frontier province excels in Rangers, scouting, irregular warfare, and dispersed fort defense; and the autonomous city concentrates the strongest single fortified harbor and professional garrison. Parent-realm regular forces remain separate and may temporarily alter the balance.

## 37. Fortification

Fortification is early modern, not medieval.

Use:

- star forts;
- bastioned citadels;
- redoubts;
- earthworks;
- coastal batteries;
- palisades;
- blockhouses;
- temporary campaign works.

Do not use castles, keeps, crenellated fantasy cities, or inherited ancient defensive works.

## 38. Warfare

The setting commonly experiences:

- small wars;
- raids;
- border disputes;
- privateering;
- punitive expeditions;
- rebellions;
- local clashes;
- covert operations and proxy struggles.

Large multi-power wars occur but are rarer and deeply disruptive.

Campaigns depend on roads, rivers, ports, magazines, wagons, bridges, weather, and seasons. Logistics should matter as much as battlefield courage.

Supernatural involvement can occasionally alter a battle, siege, campaign decision, intelligence assessment, or postwar interpretation, but it should remain exceptional rather than a routine layer of every military operation. The existence of War Witches or comparable practitioners does not imply organized battle-mage corps.

## 39. Transportation

Transportation is pre-rail and pre-steam.

People and goods move by:

- sailing ships;
- ferries;
- barges and riverboats;
- horses;
- wagons and wagon convoys;
- stagecoaches;
- post riders and relay systems;
- canals;
- bridges and toll roads.

Travel is slow, weather-dependent, expensive, and risky. Control of navigable rivers, good roads, ferries, bridges, harbors, and supply depots carries political and military importance.

# PART IX — BUILT ENVIRONMENT AND DAILY LIFE

## 40. Architectural Guardrail

There are **no castles, ancient cities, ancient ruins, lost empires, or inherited monumental structures**.

Everything visible belongs to the present civilization. Buildings may be weathered or locally old, but never ancient.

> **Buildings should look recently constructed, maintained, expanded, and actively inhabited—not ancient, monumental, abandoned, or romanticized as ruins.**

## 41. Civic and Military Architecture

Political authority is expressed through new neoclassical public buildings in white or pale stone, including columns, broad stairs, domes, assembly chambers, courts, and ceremonial halls.

Military power is expressed through practical gunpowder fortification: batteries, star forts, redoubts, earthworks, palisades, and fortified harbors.

Ports and towns contain:

- docks and wharves;
- warehouses;
- counting houses;
- markets;
- mills;
- foundries;
- workshops;
- shipyards;
- customs houses;
- courthouses;
- meetinghouses;
- taverns and inns.

## 42. Domestic Architecture

Rural settled areas commonly contain timber-framed farmhouses with clapboard or shingle siding, steep roofs, chimneys, small-paned windows, and additions constructed over time.

Outbuildings may include barns, smokehouses, workshops, dairies, stables, kitchens, and storage sheds.

Frontier homes are simpler: log or rough-timber structures, one or two rooms, large hearths, limited glass, and locally made furniture. Established frontier settlements gradually replace these with framed houses, shops, inns, and meetinghouses.

Cities and towns contain:

- narrow townhouses and row houses;
- shops with living quarters above or behind them;
- workshops opening onto the street;
- taverns and inns with public rooms, kitchens, yards, and stables;
- warehouses and counting houses near docks and rivers.

Wealthy homes are elegant but not palatial. Status appears through symmetry, brick or dressed stone, formal entrances, larger windows, gardens, imported furniture, wallpaper, porcelain, silver, clocks, books, parlors, and studies.

Interiors rely on fireplaces, candles, oil lamps, wooden furniture, rugs, curtains, chests, cupboards, and multipurpose rooms. Even wealthy homes require constant labor for heat, water, food storage, cleaning, and maintenance.

Native domestic architecture varies by nation, climate, available materials, and cultural practice rather than following a universal template.

## 43. Recurring Settlement Environments

### Major Ports

Major ports are cosmopolitan, commercial, crowded, press-driven, and politically contentious. They contain docks, warehouses, customs houses, civic centers, star forts, universities, learned societies, banks, taverns, newspapers, and dense neighborhoods.

Supernatural claims are often treated skeptically in public, but governments, universities, churches, commercial houses, and intelligence networks maintain enough experience with genuine anomalies that serious cases are investigated rather than automatically dismissed. Clandestine practice and fashionable curiosity also flourish.

### Settled Provinces

Settled provinces contain farms, market towns, county seats, mills, meetinghouses, estates, workshops, and commercial holdings. Local reputation, family networks, elections, courts, and credit shape daily life.

Folk belief and inherited cautions may be more culturally visible than in major ports. Genuine incidents remain uncommon, and disputes focus as much on explanation, legitimacy, and responsibility as on whether anything unusual occurred.

### Frontier Districts and Native Nations

Frontier districts include scattered farms, posts, forts, Rangers, traders, interpreters, guides, ferries, and mixed legal authority.

Native nations overlap geographically with the frontier but must remain conceptually distinct from it. They are complete political and cultural societies, not part of an undifferentiated wilderness.

Supernatural traditions may be more openly embedded within some native ceremonial systems, but their exact meanings and institutional roles differ by nation and must not be flattened into one pan-native practice.

## 44. Clothing

Clothing follows eighteenth-century silhouettes and materials:

- linen, wool, leather, and other natural fabrics;
- shirts, waistcoats, coats, breeches or trousers, stockings, shoes, and boots;
- skirts, bodices, aprons, caps, bonnets, gowns, mantles, and cloaks;
- broad hats, tricorns, dressed or powdered hair, neckcloths, and occupational accessories.

The world should not appear uniformly brown, gray, or impoverished. Wealth and status show through color, fabric, tailoring, cleanliness, imported detail, jewelry, seals, watches, and well-maintained clothing.

Frontier and rural dress may include hunting shirts, leggings, sturdy footwear, bags, belts, powder horns, knives, and extensive cross-cultural borrowing.

Military uniforms exist but are not perfectly standardized.

Faction colors are **player-facing card-border colors**, not mandatory clothing schemes. Members of the same faction can dress very differently.

Avoid medieval armor, generic wizard robes, oversized fantasy pauldrons, and heraldic fantasy uniforms.

## 45. Visual-Lore Continuity and Artwork Guardrails

Artwork development is a valid source of setting canon when a visual decision establishes something about the world, its people, institutions, technology, or material culture. Purely graphic decisions—card shells, typography, interface treatment, component layout, and similar presentation rules—belong in the separate visual-design documentation rather than being duplicated here.

### One Coherent Powder-and-Parchment World

Characters, places, props, and scenes should appear to belong to the same colonial and early-modern-adjacent world. Avoid generic prestige-fantasy imagery, medieval visual shorthand, industrial-era details, and arbitrary exotic additions intended only to signal that the setting is “not our America.”

Familiar eighteenth-century forms are acceptable. Avoiding literal real-world national identifiers—such as the American flag, exact state emblems, and recognizable national uniforms—is generally sufficient. Do not distort ordinary architecture, clothing, weapons, tools, or civic life merely to make the setting superficially unfamiliar.

The increased supernatural baseline should appear through occasional anomalous events, ritual traces, unusual objects, institutional precautions, disputed evidence, and rare practitioners rather than through ubiquitous glowing architecture, enchanted consumer goods, or generic fantasy scenery.

### Faction Identity in Art

Faction colors belong primarily to cards, components, emblems, and interfaces. They do not dictate character wardrobes or require everyone associated with a faction to wear matching colors.

Faction identity should instead read through:

- social role and behavior;
- setting and composition;
- tools, documents, weapons, and occupational props;
- institutional context;
- recurring motifs used with restraint.

The six factions should not look like six color-coded nations or uniformed fantasy houses.

### Leader Design Anchors

Leader titles are recurring archetypal roles, not portraits of unique historical individuals. Historical and fictional references guide posture, function, silhouette, and mode of power; they are not people to be copied directly into the setting.

Confirmed or strongly established design anchors include:

- **General:** heroic field commander mounted on a white horse; elevated, mobile, and inspirational.
- **Commandant:** on foot, severe, disciplined, and associated with forts, drill yards, garrisons, inspections, readiness, and military administration rather than battlefield glory.
- **Ambassador:** refined but approachable diplomatic operator associated with sealed correspondence, treaties, gifts, private meetings, and negotiated relationships.
- **Senator:** internal political and legal authority associated with assemblies, statutes, debate, procedure, and public legitimacy.
- **Grand Inquisitor:** institutional religious authority associated with tribunals, doctrine, warrants, judgment, and formal coercion.
- **Witch Hunter:** mobile field investigator and pursuer, visually distinct from both the Grand Inquisitor and the Ranger; the weathered Inquisition hat is an important marker.
- **Alchemist:** educated experimental scholar using laboratories, instruments, manuscripts, vessels, and quasi-forbidden natural philosophy; never a generic robed wizard.
- **Spirit Walker:** the established lean adult man with long dark hair, branching antler headdress, chalk-white body and face paint with dark red linear markings, minimal layered hide and cord garments, and an intense severe presence; never a generic “tribal shaman,” literal skinwalker, druid, or pan-native composite.
- **Ranger:** frontier intelligence officer and irregular field commander associated with long guns, maps, trails, reconnaissance, and difficult terrain.
- **Spymaster:** respectable urban elite whose covert role is hidden; associated with correspondence, agents, ciphers, surveillance, and discreet social power.
- **Banker:** counting-house financier associated with ledgers, notes, credit, insurance, public finance, and institutional trust rather than simply conspicuous wealth.
- **Executive:** chartered-company magnate whose commercial authority extends into logistics, property, administration, monopoly, and quasi-governmental power.

### Gameplay Imagery

Card art may depict situations that suggest military, political, commercial, covert, religious, or supernatural conflict without implying that every match corresponds to one fixed war or place. A card image should communicate the concept of the card and the setting, not create an unintended canonical geography.

## 46. Food and Drink

Food is grounded in the material culture of a prosperous eighteenth-century Atlantic-style world.

Common foods may include:

- breads and grain dishes;
- corn and beans;
- vegetables and orchard produce;
- meat and game;
- fish and shellfish;
- pies, puddings, and baked goods;
- stews and soups;
- preserved, salted, dried, pickled, and smoked foods.

Common drinks may include beer, ale, cider, wine, rum, brandy, tea, coffee, chocolate, and ordinary water where safe or practical.

No special prohibition on alcohol, coffee, or tea exists in the setting unless later established for a specific culture or religious community.

## 47. Civic and Social Life

Public life gathers around:

- markets;
- docks;
- mills;
- town greens;
- congregations and meetinghouses;
- taverns and inns;
- coffeehouses;
- courthouses;
- elections and councils;
- militia musters;
- fairs and races;
- lectures, public readings, and debates.

Entertainment may include music, dancing, songs, traveling performers, theater in larger cities, boxing, shooting contests, gambling, prints, caricatures, serialized stories, and sensational pamphlets.

### Holidays, Festivals, and Public Ceremonies

The calendar is punctuated by religious observances, market fairs, harvest celebrations, militia musters, civic anniversaries, military parades, public fasts or thanksgivings, and official ceremonies. Particular observances vary by polity, church, and region rather than forming one universal calendar.

These occasions combine worship, commerce, recreation, and politics. They may include sermons, proclamations, bells, music, processions, public meals, competitions, illuminations, speeches, and the ceremonial appearance of officials or military units. A modest town can become crowded and vibrant when surrounding households gather for a fair, election, court session, muster, or holy day.

Ceremonies honoring a parent realm, sovereign, governor, military victory, or charter remain common in many jurisdictions. As resentment toward distant rule grows, participation in such observances—and the language used in sermons, speeches, loyal addresses, and public toasts—can become politically contentious. Local civic anniversaries and commemorations may increasingly compete with parent-realm celebrations for public loyalty.

### Voluntary Associations

Voluntary associations are an important layer of civic life between the household and formal government. Depending on the place, they may include:

- fire companies;
- charitable and mutual-aid societies;
- trade and professional associations;
- veterans’ groups and militia-related societies;
- political clubs and committees;
- debating, literary, scientific, dining, and social societies;
- burial societies, neighborhood organizations, and relief funds.

These organizations raise money, provide practical services, sponsor lectures or publications, assist members in hardship, organize public events, and create durable networks of reputation and patronage. Some are broadly civic, while others are tied to a church, trade, neighborhood, factional interest, or political cause. They can cooperate with governments without being governmental institutions themselves.

Voluntary associations help make even modest towns socially dense and politically active. They also provide natural points of contact with the six factions without implying that every useful institution belongs directly to a faction.

Reputation is a major social currency.

## 48. Public Works and Infrastructure

Public works are financed and maintained through a mixed, decentralized system rather than a single modern infrastructure authority. Depending on the jurisdiction and project, support may come from:

- provincial or municipal appropriations and local taxes;
- tolls, port fees, market fees, and other user charges;
- chartered companies granted the right to construct and operate roads, bridges, canals, ferries, or wharves;
- subscriptions and contributions from merchants, landowners, congregations, and voluntary associations;
- locally required labor or material contributions, especially for roads and minor works;
- occasional lotteries, special levies, or emergency collections for major projects.

Roads, bridges, ferries, canals, docks, markets, drainage, fire protection, and civic buildings therefore vary greatly in quality. Wealthy ports and commercially important corridors may possess impressive facilities, while rural roads remain seasonal, poorly maintained, or dependent on local labor. Rivers and coastal shipping remain the most reliable means of moving heavy goods.

Responsibility is often divided among assemblies, counties, municipalities, chartered companies, private proprietors, and local associations. Disputes over tolls, maintenance, land acquisition, monopoly privileges, and who benefits from a project are routine. Infrastructure is both a practical necessity and a major field of political and financial influence.

There is no universal central public-works ministry.

### Taxation and Public Finance

Governments raise revenue through a varied mix of customs duties, excises, land and property levies, poll or household taxes, licenses, court and recording fees, port and market charges, rents or payments attached to chartered land, and occasional special assessments. There is no modern general income-tax system. The exact balance differs sharply by polity: commercial ports rely heavily on customs and harbor revenue, while agricultural jurisdictions depend more on land, property, and locally collected levies.

Tax collection is decentralized and often politically contentious. Assemblies commonly authorize taxation and appropriations, while governors, treasurers, customs officers, sheriffs, municipalities, and contracted collectors administer particular revenues. Taxpayers may sometimes satisfy obligations through recognized commodities, notes, or locally accepted instruments where coin is scarce. Arrears, exemptions, disputed valuations, evasion, smuggling, and accusations of favoritism are common.

Governments also finance wars, forts, infrastructure, and emergencies through borrowing. They may issue bonds, bills, treasury notes, or other promises backed by future taxes, customs receipts, land sales, or institutional credit. Chartered banks, wealthy merchants, financiers, and overseas lenders therefore possess substantial political influence. Lotteries and public subscriptions may fund particular civic projects or institutions.

Public treasuries are rarely flush with ready money. Governments may owe soldiers, contractors, suppliers, and officeholders for long periods, and the value of public paper depends heavily on political confidence. Debates over taxation are therefore also debates over consent, representation, public credit, and which regions or social groups bear the cost of government.

# PART X — THE UNNAMED RIVER-PORT PROVINCE

## 49. Development Status

This province and city are a representative case study intended to make the setting concrete. They remain **unnamed** by deliberate choice.

No proper names should be assigned until their character is sufficiently developed.

## 50. Founding and Historical Identity

The city began as one of the earliest charter settlements: broadly comparable to a hypothetical Jamestown that survived, prospered, and developed continuously into a major colonial city.

It is:

- Jamestown-like in origin and historical identity;
- comparable to New York or Philadelphia in eventual urban scale and influence;
- an early foothold founded by a chartered venture;
- positioned on a navigable tidal river, some distance upriver from the open coast;
- protected from direct naval attack while remaining accessible to ocean-going vessels;
- the principal city of an old and prosperous river province.

Its founding status matters deeply to local identity, but it is not the capital, birthplace, or unquestioned center of the entire setting.

## 51. City and Province

The city is:

- commercial;
- cosmopolitan;
- crowded;
- argumentative;
- politically volatile;
- conscious of its age and founding importance.

The surrounding province is:

- agricultural;
- locally oriented;
- strongly attached to county and family identity;
- suspicious that port merchants and financiers dominate provincial policy.

Both share a strong provincial identity and treat the parent realm’s authority as important but remote.

Nearby native nations remain meaningful trading partners and diplomatic powers, not merely obstacles to expansion.

### Relations with Nearby Native Nations

The province's principal native counterpart is the **riverine paramountcy**, within whose political territory the original settler foothold was established. Their relationship combines centuries of trade, diplomacy, intermarriage, warfare, separate agreements with constituent communities, disputed jurisdiction, and incompatible accounts of the province's founding.

The immediate postwar pressure is the upper-river wartime corridor. The paramountcy regards provincial forts, routes, and supporting uses as temporary permissions, while provincial institutions increasingly claim lasting rights based on continued occupation, investment, military service, and settlement. Other neighboring nations, particular treaties, local alliances, and the identities of affected border communities remain unresolved.

### Inland Frontier

The province’s inland districts reflect the setting-wide frontier pattern described in Part III: overlapping settlements, paramountcy territory, disputed claims, forts, posts, private land companies, and incomplete government control.

The most immediate crisis lies along an upper-river corridor opened for limited wartime defense and now contested through remaining garrisons, veteran and civilian settlement, provincial surveys and grants, expelled surveyors, dismantled improvements, livestock disputes, and competing patrols. The corridor's exact geography, posts, communities, legal instruments, and current incidents remain **deliberately unresolved**.

## 52. Economic Foundation

The province’s fertile river interior made the city wealthy.

Agricultural exports move downstream to be:

- inspected;
- graded;
- stored;
- processed;
- financed;
- insured;
- shipped overseas.

This trade supports warehouses, mills, shipyards, merchant houses, banks, insurers, brokers, and transport networks.

## 53. Tobacco

Tobacco is the province’s defining high-value export and the crop that first made it rich.

The economy is **tobacco-led but not plantation-defined**.

Production relies mainly on:

- independent growers;
- family farms;
- tenant farms;
- wage and seasonal labor;
- contracted workers;
- larger estates organized without hereditary chattel slavery.

The broader economy also includes grain, timber, livestock, shipbuilding, preserved food, and other exports, preventing total dependence on one crop.

Tobacco creates structural pressures:

- soil exhaustion and movement upriver;
- land speculation;
- credit advanced against future harvests;
- dependence on inspection warehouses and shipping;
- volatile overseas demand;
- mutual dependence and resentment between growers and merchants.

> **Tobacco made the province rich, but it did not determine its entire labor system.**

## 54. Central Political Rivalry

Provincial politics are shaped by conflict between two mutually dependent interests.

### The Landed Interest

The landed interest includes:

- established tobacco growers;
- county officials;
- rural lawyers;
- prominent families;
- other agricultural property holders.

It generally favors:

- low land taxes;
- access to new acreage;
- protection for growers;
- strong county government;
- resistance to merchant control.

It often views urban creditors as profiting from the labor and risk of rural producers.

### The Commercial Interest

The commercial interest includes:

- merchants;
- bankers;
- insurers;
- shipowners;
- warehouse operators;
- brokers;
- urban lawyers and professionals.

It generally favors:

- reliable debt enforcement;
- improved roads, docks, and harbors;
- standardized inspection;
- stable currency and negotiable credit;
- trade policies favorable to shipping.

It often views rural leaders as indebted, parochial, and resistant to necessary reform.

Neither side can dominate permanently. Growers need credit, transport, and overseas markets; merchants need tobacco and cooperation from the countryside.

## 55. Provincial Government

The province is governed through a durable balance between an appointed governor and an elected assembly.

### Governor

The governor is formally appointed under the old charter, likely by the parent realm or its delegated authority.

The governor oversees:

- senior appointments;
- militia commissions;
- external negotiations;
- official correspondence;
- executive enforcement;
- some patronage and ceremonial authority.

### Assembly

The elected assembly controls:

- taxation;
- appropriations;
- infrastructure funding;
- much provincial legislation;
- the money required to carry out executive policy.

The governor may veto, delay, withhold appointments, or appeal to the parent realm. The assembly may refuse funding, attach conditions, obstruct executive programs, or use investigations and public pressure.

> **The governor possesses formal executive authority, but the elected assembly controls the money needed to exercise it.**

## 56. Provincial Representation

Rural districts possess disproportionate legislative influence despite the port city’s growing population and economic importance.

This imbalance is an enduring urban grievance. It does not leave the city powerless, because the port controls money, information, organization, printing, and commercial connections.

## 57. Provincial Franchise

Political participation resembles early America rather than universal suffrage.

Most free adult men who own sufficient property or meet an equivalent tax qualification may vote. This includes many independent farmers, merchants, shopkeepers, and tradesmen.

Those commonly excluded include:

- women;
- servants and most contracted laborers;
- landless laborers;
- many tenants;
- dependents without an independent household;
- people disqualified under local law by debt or criminal judgment.

Rural qualifications often emphasize land. Urban qualifications may recognize taxable property, business ownership, or membership in particular chartered trades.

Holding office generally requires more property, standing, education, and local connection than voting.

The political culture sincerely values representation and the rights of independent citizens, while defining “independent” narrowly enough to preserve hierarchy.

## 58. Elections

Elections are public civic events.

Campaigns involve:

- speeches;
- handbills;
- newspaper essays;
- tavern gatherings;
- endorsements;
- personal visits;
- family and patronage networks;
- public hospitality and election “treating.”

The province uses **viva voce voting**. Qualified voters appear publicly at the courthouse or polling place and declare their choices aloud before officials, candidates, neighbors, creditors, employers, and family acquaintances.

Votes are genuinely contested but never private.

> **Everyone knows who voted, how they voted, and whom they disappointed.**

Formal parties may not yet exist, but recognizable alliances, political circles, and recurring coalitions do.

## 59. City Government

The city is governed under its own charter by an elected municipal council.

The council oversees:

- docks and markets;
- streets and sanitation;
- fire prevention;
- municipal taxes and fees;
- licenses for taverns, ferries, warehouses, and trades;
- the night watch and local officers;
- public buildings and harbor facilities.

Councilors are elected by qualified urban voters through wards.

The mayor is selected indirectly by the elected council from among its members and must maintain a governing coalition.

The city government is more commercially oriented than the provincial assembly, but it is not a self-perpetuating oligarchy.

## 60. Ward Representation

Municipal representation is ward-based and only imperfectly follows population.

Ward boundaries and seat allocations are fixed by charter and adjusted slowly. Older wards retain influence while newer dockside and working neighborhoods become crowded without immediately receiving proportional representation.

The system is:

> **Representative, but not proportionally representative.**

Creating a new ward, redrawing boundaries, or adding council seats can become a major political conflict involving both the city and provincial institutions.

## 61. Public Order in the City

The city relies on:

- magistrates;
- constables;
- ward officers;
- a part-time night watch.

There is no unified professional police force.

The watch patrols streets, raises fire and disturbance alarms, enforces curfews and municipal regulations, and detains obvious offenders. Constables carry out warrants and court orders.

Investigation depends heavily on victims, witnesses, informants, private rewards, newspaper notices, and interested officials.

## 62. Architecture and Defense

The harbor is protected by coastal batteries and a star fort rather than medieval walls.

Civic authority appears through pale neoclassical public buildings, while the working city is dominated by wharves, warehouses, shipyards, markets, workshops, counting houses, taverns, row houses, and crowded streets.

The oldest surviving structures are colonial-era buildings from the city’s own settlement history, not relics of an ancient civilization.

## 63. City Courts and Legal Practice

The city uses a layered court system:

- ward magistrates and justices of the peace handle minor disputes, public-order offenses, licensing violations, small debts, and preliminary hearings;
- a municipal court hears substantial civil cases, commercial disputes, property claims, and ordinary criminal trials arising within the city;
- provincial superior courts hear major felonies, appeals, politically sensitive cases, and disputes involving provincial law or multiple jurisdictions;
- a specialized admiralty or maritime court handles shipping contracts, seizures, salvage, customs violations, privateering disputes, and offenses committed at sea.

Juries are common in ordinary courts, with eligibility shaped by property, reputation, and social standing. The admiralty court may operate without a jury, making it a recurring source of political resentment.

Lawyers are central to urban life. They represent merchants, shipowners, creditors, defendants, landowners, companies, and political institutions; draft contracts, wills, charters, and petitions; and frequently serve in the assembly or municipal government. The city’s commercial importance and overlapping jurisdictions make legal interpretation a major source of power.

The broader setting-wide legal baseline in Part VII applies here: lawyers commonly read law through apprenticeship, inherited common law is modified locally, case reporting is incomplete, public prosecution is emerging, and debt, punishment, insolvency, and judicial independence vary by jurisdiction. The city’s specific departures from that shared pattern have not yet been defined.

# PART XI — THE UNNAMED MARITIME COMMERCIAL COMMONWEALTH

## 64. Development Status

This polity is a second representative case study intended to distinguish the settler world’s regional societies from one another. It remains **unnamed** by deliberate choice, and its constitution, cities, founding history, and exact geography should not be fixed until they are developed incrementally.

## 65. Baseline Character

The polity is a **densely settled maritime commercial commonwealth** organized around several excellent harbors, a network of prosperous coastal towns, and extensive overseas and inter-polity commerce. Unlike the tobacco-rich river province, it is not dominated by one great agricultural interior or a single export staple.

Its economy and public life center on:

- shipping and the carrying trade;
- fishing and preserved maritime goods;
- shipbuilding, ropewalks, sail lofts, chandlers, and repair trades;
- merchants, insurers, brokers, creditors, and maritime lawyers;
- skilled urban and coastal trades;
- overseas trade, coastal shipping, privateering, and widespread smuggling.

Compared with the river province and the Inquisition stronghold, it is generally:

- more urban and commercially dispersed;
- more dependent on strong town and municipal institutions;
- more religiously pluralistic by settler standards;
- more heavily influenced by merchants, shipowners, insurers, and skilled tradespeople;
- especially suspicious of monopolies, navigation restrictions, customs interference, and policies that obstruct maritime commerce.

One port may be the largest city and principal commercial center, but several secondary ports remain wealthy, politically important, and capable of resisting complete metropolitan domination. The commonwealth should therefore feel like a **network of maritime communities** rather than one capital ruling an undifferentiated hinterland.

### Government and Local Autonomy

Its historical comparison is **Rhode Island in maritime economy, pluralism, and political temperament, combined with Connecticut in governmental organization and town-based administration**. The commonwealth has an **elected governor and representative assembly**, rather than an executive appointed routinely from overseas. Its old charter grants unusually broad local self-government under nominal parent-realm allegiance.

Individual towns retain extensive authority over local taxation, harbors, roads, ferries, markets, schools, militias, religious arrangements, poor relief, and ordinary administration. The commonwealth government coordinates wider courts, defense, inter-polity relations, trade policy, and matters affecting several towns. Because political and commercial power is distributed among multiple ports, governing normally requires coalitions rather than simple obedience to one metropolitan center.

Representation in the commonwealth assembly is organized principally by **chartered town rather than equal population districts**. Each recognized town sends one or more representatives under rules fixed by charter and later statute. As ports and newer settlements grow at different rates, representation does not adjust automatically: older and smaller towns may retain disproportionate influence, while larger ports press periodically for additional seats or reapportionment. The system is genuinely representative, but its town-based structure favors historic communities and makes political reform a recurring subject of negotiation.

### Seat of Government

No single port should effortlessly dominate the commonwealth's political life. The seat of government may rotate among established towns or be distributed through institutions whose exact arrangement remains unresolved. This reinforces the commonwealth's identity as a compact among strong municipalities rather than a miniature centralized province.

### Maritime Capacity

The commonwealth possesses the strongest maritime capacity under local settler control, although it does not maintain a permanent navy comparable to a parent realm. Its sailors, shipyards, captains, merchants, insurers, financiers, armed merchantmen, coastal craft, and privateering tradition allow it to convert commercial strength into wartime sea power quickly.

It excels at convoy organization, maritime intelligence, troop and supply movement, ship repair, financing, and coordination among ports. Its smaller land-force pool and the reluctance of commercial interests to disrupt trade limit its capacity for prolonged inland warfare.

### Political Tension Among Ports and Towns

The largest ports favor infrastructure, credit, maritime regulation, and representation commensurate with their population and economic contribution. Older and smaller towns defend chartered representation, local autonomy, and institutional protections that prevent the largest ports from dominating the commonwealth. They may distrust metropolitan merchants, oppose taxes for distant harbor projects, resist centralized regulation, and argue that the commonwealth is a union of self-governing towns rather than a population-weighted commercial state.

The disagreement cuts across social and economic groups rather than creating two permanent parties. Port merchants may ally with growing secondary towns, while artisans or neighborhood leaders inside the largest ports may resist policies favored by their own commercial elite. Reapportionment, infrastructure spending, customs policy, public debt, and the scope of town authority therefore become recurring subjects of coalition politics.

Its exact founding history, franchise, detailed legislative procedure, terms of office, identities of the towns among which government rotates, principal ports, denominational composition, specific overseas disputes, the later evolution of the port-town conflict, the scale and organization of any permanent public maritime service, and the identities and treaty histories of its neighboring native nations remain unresolved.

# PART XII — THE UNNAMED CHARTERED COMPANY TERRITORY

## 66. Development Status

This polity is a third representative case study intended to establish the strongest institutional setting for chartered enterprise, corporate government, and the **Executive** archetype. It remains **unnamed** by deliberate choice. Its exact geography, settlements, parent realm, corporate constitution, and degree of later political reform remain unresolved.

## 67. Historical and Institutional Baseline

The territory is best understood as a **mature settler colony in which a joint-stock company retained its chartered governing authority instead of surrendering it to the parent realm**.

Its historical comparison combines:

- a **Virginia Company-like origin**, because it was established to found and govern a settler society rather than merely operate remote trading posts;
- a **New Netherland-like commercial organization**, in which trade, land grants, private investors, and corporate concessions shape settlement;
- a **Hudson’s Bay Company-like territorial authority**, allowing the company to function as government, dominant landlord, and principal economic institution in some districts;
- limited **East India Company-like powers** only at the upper edge of its authority, such as appointing governors, administering courts, maintaining forts and armed forces, or exercising civil and military jurisdiction under charter.

The territory should not become a continental empire or a purely extractive trading enclave. It is a settled, multi-generational society with towns, farms, workshops, churches, courts, families, and local political life. What distinguishes it is that the company remains embedded in nearly every layer of government and commerce.

The central premise is:

> **A mature American-style colony in which the company never ceased to be the state.**

## 68. Broad Character

### Commercial Foundation and Growth

The company survived because its original charter gave it control of a **major inland timber corridor**. Extensive forests supplied valuable construction lumber, ship timbers, masts, and related materials, while navigable rivers allowed logs and rough-cut timber to move toward company ports, sawmills, warehouses, and shipyards. The company founded forts, roads, river landings, interior posts, and settlements to secure cutting rights, regulate transport, finance extraction, and channel the trade through its own facilities. Early settlement therefore followed the timber corridor rather than spreading evenly across the territory.

As permanent settlement expanded, the territory diversified into farming, local manufacturing, shipping, land development, finance, naval stores, furs, and other forms of trade. Timber remains central to the company's identity, privileges, infrastructure, and political claims, but the mature territory is no longer dependent on one export alone.

The exact route, forest region, and geography remain deliberately unresolved.

### Timber Rights, Title, and Native Jurisdiction

The company maintains its forest claims through several overlapping and sometimes contradictory sources of title:

- the broad territorial charter granted by its parent realm;
- treaties, purchases, leases, and timber concessions negotiated with particular native nations;
- surveys, grants, licenses, and proprietary estates later issued by the company itself;
- customary cutting and access rights claimed by settlers, workers, and local communities.

Some of these claims are legitimate and mutually recognized. Others remain disputed because the parent realm granted territory it did not control, negotiators understood agreements differently, boundaries were poorly surveyed, or company officials expanded limited cutting rights into claims of permanent ownership or jurisdiction. Not every dispute is an innocent misunderstanding: particular executives, surveyors, or local officers may knowingly exploit ambiguity, political pressure, debt, or military advantage to enlarge company concessions. Others honor agreements because predictable title and stable native relations are essential to long-term trade.

The company regulates timber through licensed crews, surveyors, branded timber marks, river checkpoints, transport permits, patrols, and company courts. Native governments may likewise regulate access, collect tolls, inspect crews, seize illegally harvested timber, or expel operators who exceed agreed boundaries.

The recurring conflict is not simply ownership in the abstract. The company tends to understand forest rights as surveyed property, transferable concessions, and commercial inventory. Native governments may understand access as a continuing political relationship involving territory, season, use, reciprocity, and renewed obligation. The resulting disputes involve sovereignty, jurisdiction, and treaty interpretation as much as lumber.

Beyond timber disputes, relations with neighboring native nations are generally adversarial and shaped by sustained corporate expansion. Trade, treaties, interpreters, guides, and temporary alliances remain important, but company surveys, debt, roads, forts, settlement, and security patrols repeatedly convert negotiated access into wider territorial claims. Native governments may answer through restrictions, seizures, expulsions, raids, and armed resistance, while the company may use punitive expeditions, manipulated rivalries, coercive credit, and favored leaders to protect or enlarge its position. Cooperation therefore persists mainly through necessity and unequal bargaining rather than durable trust.

### Directors, Shareholders, and the Corporate Center

The company is governed by a **divided board containing both overseas and resident interests**. Most shares—and therefore the formal voting majority—remain concentrated among investors, merchant houses, and financial institutions in the parent realm. The company’s principal board and shareholder meetings are consequently still centered overseas.

The territory is no longer governed entirely by people who have never lived there. Long-established local magnates, major timber and shipping interests, former governors, and resident shareholders hold substantial blocks of stock and occupy recognized seats on the board. Some directors divide their time between the territory and the parent realm, while resident directors maintain agents and voting proxies overseas.

This creates two broad, overlapping interests rather than fixed parties:

- **Overseas directors** tend to emphasize dividends, charter protection, parent-realm connections, and the value of the company’s monopoly privileges.
- **Resident directors** tend to emphasize infrastructure, defense, settlement, stable native relations, local credit, and the long-term prosperity of the territory.

Both groups contain opportunists and public-minded figures. The board’s central political conflict is therefore not simply foreign exploitation against local virtue, but disagreement over whether the territory exists primarily as an investment, a public trust, or a mature society with rights of its own.

The **Executive** archetype most naturally represents a managing director, governor-director, or dominant resident magnate capable of translating corporate ownership into administrative and territorial power.

### Civil Government and Corporate Constitution

The territory uses a **mixed corporate–representative government**. The company appoints the governor or chief executive, an executive council, senior judges, military commanders, surveyors, and other high officers. These officials administer the charter, protect the company’s concessions, manage relations with the parent realm, and oversee the territory’s strategic infrastructure and security.

Settlers elect a representative assembly from chartered towns, districts, or qualifying freeholders. The assembly possesses genuine legislative authority over local taxation, appropriations, roads, ordinary regulation, petitions, and many matters of civil government. It may investigate officials, negotiate public spending, and obstruct policies by withholding locally raised revenue. Local towns and courts retain additional powers according to their grants and customs.

The assembly is not sovereign. Company directors or the governor may veto legislation that conflicts with the charter, corporate privileges, or parent-realm instructions. The company retains privileged authority over land grants, timber concessions, major trade regulation, senior appointments, forts, and strategic routes. Courts therefore combine company-appointed judges with locally chosen magistrates and juries.

This arrangement creates a durable constitutional conflict. Settlers regard representation and local taxation as established rights, while the company argues that its capital, charter, and continuing obligations entitle it to final control over the territory’s strategic assets. Disputes regularly concern dividends versus public investment, monopoly privilege, land and timber policy, taxation, appointments, and whether directors who may live overseas can legitimately govern a mature society.

### Company Security and Territorial Force

The territory relies primarily on a **company-employed security apparatus** rather than on ordinary provincial forces. The company maintains professional guards, forest wardens, river patrols, checkpoint officers, fortified-post garrisons, and armed detachments assigned to timber concessions, warehouses, yards, roads, and other strategic facilities.

Their authority is quasi-public because the company itself is the chartered government. Within the territory, recognized company officers may detain suspects, seize unlawfully harvested goods, enforce court orders, and call upon local officials for assistance. Their jurisdiction is nevertheless contested wherever corporate property enforcement overlaps with ordinary civil law, municipal authority, private freehold land, or native sovereignty.

Local militias, town watches, constables, and sheriffs still exist. They handle ordinary public order, community defense, and locally directed emergencies, while the company security establishment concentrates on strategic infrastructure, charter privileges, the timber corridor, and corporate interests.

Company security is better trained, equipped, and continuously employed than most local forces, but it is not an enormous standing army.

### Capital and Municipal Government

One dominant company-founded capital and port concentrates the territorial government, company headquarters, senior courts, finance, customs, timber yards, sawmills, shipyards, warehouses, and overseas trade. Secondary ports, interior towns, and river settlements remain economically important, but none rivals the capital's combined political, financial, and maritime weight.

The capital has an **elected municipal council**, usually chosen by wards or other chartered districts from among qualified property-holding residents. The council controls ordinary streets, markets, sanitation, fire prevention, local watch functions, neighborhood improvements, and municipal taxation. It may select the mayor or chief magistrate from among its own members.

The company retains separate chartered authority over the principal wharves, timber yards, customs facilities, major warehouses, survey offices, and other strategic property. The city government is therefore **genuinely elected but structurally constrained** where municipal authority touches corporate property, charter privilege, or territorial trade regulation.

### Land, Credit, and Dependency

The company is the territory’s largest landholder, employer, creditor, and infrastructure provider, but most residents are not company employees. Independent farms, workshops, merchants, churches, professions, and self-governing towns operate within the corporate order.

Land tenure is mixed. Older settled districts contain substantial freehold ownership, while newer districts, strategic corridors, and timber regions remain more heavily leasehold or concession-based under company control. Company influence is felt through rents, credit, licenses, land grants, stores, tolls, courts, and access to roads, rivers, mills, warehouses, and ports.

Recurring conflict concerns rents, fees, debt terms, exclusive purchasing or shipping arrangements, company stores and tied credit, access to infrastructure, timber and grazing rights, and whether public works should serve the territory broadly or protect company revenue first.

### Religion

The territory is religiously pluralistic by settler standards. No strong territory-wide establishment governs worship or political rights, although the parent realm’s church may retain ceremonial prestige and advantages in company patronage. The company generally favors toleration because immigration, skilled labor, investment, and commercial stability matter more to it than denominational uniformity. The Inquisition has no recognized jurisdiction there.

### Relationship with the Parent Realm

The parent realm generally **protects the company’s charter because the arrangement remains useful**. The company supplies strategically valuable timber, maintains forts and infrastructure, settles territory, administers distant districts, and provides revenue without requiring the parent government to build a large colonial bureaucracy of its own.

The arrangement is not permanent by nature. Scandal, poor performance, political rivalry, or a change in imperial priorities can produce investigation, revision, direct intervention, or threats of revocation. The company therefore portrays itself both as a loyal imperial instrument and as a shield protecting settlers from more intrusive direct rule.

Politically, the territory is more cautious than leading centers of continental opposition because its charter, credit, monopoly privileges, and governing authority depend heavily on parent-realm protection. Overseas directors usually favor loyalty and negotiation, while resident directors, elected representatives, freeholders, and independent merchants may resist particular taxes, trade restrictions, appointments, or charter interference.

The territory is therefore neither a conventional free province nor a single company settlement. It is a mature civil society living inside a corporate constitutional order whose privileges remain extensive enough to shape opportunity, dependency, and political power.

The territory's detailed franchise, legislative and municipal procedure, exact division of authority over particular offices and facilities, identities and internal politics of neighboring native nations, histories and boundaries of individual concessions, and precise later political trajectory remain open.

# PART XIII — THE UNNAMED FRONTIER PROVINCE

## 69. Development Status

This is a **representative polity under active development**, not a template for every frontier district in the setting. It is formally claimed by the principal rival parent realm, but its proper name, exact geography and disputed boundaries, settlements, native neighbors, political offices, and detailed history remain unresolved.

## 70. Historical and Institutional Baseline

The closest historical comparison is a blend of **colonial North Carolina, western Virginia and the Ohio Country, and colonial Georgia**:

- **North Carolina's backcountry** provides the principal political and social model: rapid inland settlement outpacing effective administration, uncertain land titles, inadequate representation, distant or corrupt officials, limited courts and law enforcement, and growing conflict between established government and backcountry communities.
- **Western Virginia and the Ohio Country** provide the strongest model for land companies, speculative grants, overlapping claims, unauthorized settlement, Rangers, forts, and strategic river valleys connecting provincial ambition to native territory and rival parent-realm interests.
- **Colonial Georgia** provides the province's strategically exposed character: a comparatively new polity reliant on fortified settlements, militia, Rangers, and difficult diplomacy with powerful neighboring native nations and rival overseas powers.

The result is essentially **a frontier polity whose backcountry conditions define the province as a whole**, rather than a mature coastal province with a remote frontier attached to it. It may possess an established capital, several settled counties, and recognized civil institutions, but much of its population growth, land speculation, military importance, and political conflict lies in newer inland districts.

Its defining tension is between **settlement and authority**. Settlers, traders, and land companies move faster than the provincial government can survey claims, honor treaties, establish courts, build roads, or provide defense. Provincial officials, local magistrates, militia officers, Rangers, company agents, native governments, and fortified communities therefore exercise overlapping practical authority.

### Boundaries and Rival Claims

The province’s formal boundaries are not a settled fact. The principal rival realm claims a broad jurisdiction around its forts, settlements, waterways, and diplomatic relationships, but the old river province and maritime commonwealth issue overlapping western grants and support land companies, surveyors, traders, and migrants entering the same region. The chartered territory also pursues commercial access through the province, while the inland native confederacy rejects the premise that rival settler charters can divide its homelands.

Effective control therefore follows towns, forts, roads, river crossings, trading relationships, and local alliances more closely than any agreed border. Competing maps are political instruments as much as geographic records.

### Relationship with the Chartered Territory

The province is **commercially dependent on and politically resentful of the chartered territory**. Company-controlled waterways, warehouses, credit, shipping, licenses, interpreters, and supply contracts connect many frontier communities to regional markets and keep distant forts provisioned.

At the same time, provincial officials, land companies, soldiers, and settlers repeatedly challenge monopoly pricing, transport charges, company restrictions, and corporate claims over routes or land grants. They argue that the province bears the military danger, settlement pressure, and diplomatic consequences of inland expansion while the company captures disproportionate profit.

### Relationship with the Inland Confederacy

The province and inland confederacy occupy an especially complicated relationship. Their geopolitical interests can align against the same parent-realm rivalries or third-party threats, and they may cooperate through trade, intelligence, military coordination, or diplomacy. Yet both also contest land, routes, forts, hunting grounds, settlement, and jurisdiction in the same frontier space.

They should therefore be capable of genuine alliance without that alliance erasing their territorial conflict. They may be coalition partners and rivals at the same time.

### Settlement and Government

The province has one modest established capital in its older governed district. It is administrative rather than metropolitan: the seat of the governor, assembly, superior court, land office, records, and arsenal, but modest beside the major cities of the older polities.

Beyond it lie courthouse villages, market towns, forts, trading posts, river landings, ferries, mills, roadside inns, and rapidly growing settlements that may exist for years before receiving formal recognition. Settlements and posts can rise or decline quickly as routes, wars, land claims, and trade change.

The province formally has an appointed governor and elected assembly. Courts, records, established merchants, creditors, land speculators, and political families are concentrated in the older settled districts. Inland communities are commonly underrepresented and may wait years for recognized counties, courts, roads, militia support, or legislative seats.

Government is distant across much of the interior. Courts may sit irregularly and officers may lack manpower. Frontier justice can depend on local magistrates, respected neighbors, militia officers, ad hoc posses, restitution, arbitration, expulsion, and community judgment. This is not an absence of law, but it produces recurring vigilantism, feuds, selective enforcement, and summary violence.

### Economy

The dispersed economy combines independent farms and livestock with hunting, trapping, forest products, inland trade, transport, skilled repair, military supply, and frontier services.

Characteristic occupations include hunters, trappers, guides, scouts, interpreters, surveyors, ferrymen, wagoners, blacksmiths, gunsmiths, millers, innkeepers, traders, and land agents. Cash scarcity makes credit, barter, store accounts, commodity payment, and seasonal labor common.

Land speculation remains economically important, but the province should not feel as though everyone is either a farmer or a speculator. Its economy is an active web of small production, trade, transport, skilled repair, military supply, and extraction.

### Relations with Native Nations

Relations with neighboring native nations are situational rather than uniformly hostile or cooperative. Trade, guiding, intelligence, diplomacy, military alliance, and shared opposition to a third party may produce close practical cooperation in one district or season, while unauthorized settlement, hunting trespass, disputed boundaries, land speculation, retaliation, and jurisdictional conflict produce violence elsewhere.

A small but recognized number of settlers leave settler society and enter native communities through marriage, adoption, kinship, long residence, captivity followed by a choice to remain, or deliberate rejection of settler life. They may become genuine members and political partisans of their adopted nations rather than neutral cultural intermediaries.

### Political Temperament

The province's political culture is strongly **localist, suspicious of distant authority, and difficult to organize from above**. Frontier residents commonly believe that the provincial capital and parent realm demand taxes, military service, fees, compliance with land rulings, and obedience to trade restrictions while providing too little protection, infrastructure, representation, or access to courts in return.

This resentment does not automatically produce ideological separatism. Many residents still claim the rights of provincial subjects and may remain loyal to familiar institutions, especially when those institutions defend local land, trade, or security. Political coordination tends to arise through county meetings, petitions, militia networks, taverns, traders, itinerant lawyers, and temporary popular movements rather than stable centralized organizations.

### Religious Practice

The province formally recognizes the principal rival realm's established church, whose clergy, chaplains, missionaries, schools, and charitable institutions are most visible in the capital, older settlements, and major forts. In everyday practice, however, much of the settler population is only nominally affiliated and **largely nonreligious or lightly observant**.

Many residents still seek baptism, marriage, burial, seasonal ceremonies, or a clergyman during crisis without treating religion as a central discipline of ordinary life. Folk beliefs, omens, charms, and fear of supernatural danger may be widespread even where formal observance is weak. Devout congregations, missionaries, and religiously serious settlements exist, but they are notable communities within a generally indifferent population rather than the provincial norm.

This practical irreligion sharpens suspicion of the Inquisition, which many frontier settlers regard less as a rival denomination than as intrusive fanaticism backed by foreign jurisdiction.

### Defense, Rangers, and Fortified Posts

The province relies chiefly on **local militias, Rangers, fortified posts, and temporary provincial expeditions**, supported by only a small body of regular provincial troops. Permanent professionals are concentrated at the capital, principal arsenals, and a limited number of strategic forts; most districts cannot expect a standing force to remain nearby.

Militia companies are organized locally and vary widely in training, equipment, discipline, and willingness to serve far from home. They are effective for immediate community defense, alarms, escorts, and short campaigns, but difficult to sustain in prolonged operations.

**Rangers are especially important** because they can operate beyond the reliable reach of roads, courts, and conventional formations. They scout, map routes, carry intelligence, screen settlements, guide regular troops, maintain contact with allied or neutral communities, pursue raiders, and conduct limited reconnaissance or raiding missions. Their frontier knowledge gives them unusual practical authority, but they are military and intelligence specialists rather than ordinary police or unquestioned civil magistrates.

Fortified posts, blockhouses, stockades, river forts, and defended trading stations secure crossings, roads, valleys, supply depots, and exposed settlements. They also serve as refuges, communication points, and symbols of provincial claims. Some are maintained by the province, some by local communities or land companies, and others by parent-realm forces, producing recurring disputes over command, expense, and jurisdiction.

The province can assemble substantial force during a crisis, but its defense remains **locally rooted, logistically fragile, and politically negotiated** rather than permanently centralized.

The exact franchise, apportionment rules, terms of office, executive powers, legislative procedures, principal settlements, precise commodity mix, organized political blocs, neighboring native nations, particular disputed claims, and precise constitutional relationship with the principal rival realm remain open for later development.

# PART XIV — THE UNNAMED AUTONOMOUS CITY

## 71. Development Status

This polity is a fifth representative case study intended to establish how a city-centered jurisdiction can function as a major settler polity in its own right. It remains **unnamed** by deliberate choice. Its precise charter, offices, franchise, institutions, neighborhoods, dependencies, parent realm, and political history remain open for incremental development.

## 72. Historical and Institutional Baseline

The polity is a **major port governed under its own charter**, politically separate from the surrounding territorial provinces rather than serving as the capital of one of them. Its jurisdiction centers on the city and harbor and may include nearby islands, defensive works, subordinate settlements, and a narrow rural hinterland, but it does not govern a broad continental interior. It maintains a direct constitutional relationship with the diminished third parent realm.

Its historical comparison combines several Atlantic-world precedents rather than reproducing one city exactly:

- **Newport** provides the principal social and commercial character: maritime, prosperous, religiously pluralistic by settler standards, politically independent-minded, and disproportionately influential for its size.
- **Colonial New York City** provides the model for unusually extensive municipal charter powers over waterfront property, wharves, docks, ferries, markets, harbor improvements, and related sources of revenue.
- **New Haven Colony** provides the city-centered political independence: a compact jurisdiction organized around one leading city and a limited collection of dependent settlements or claims, surviving as a mature polity rather than being absorbed by a neighboring province.
- **St. Eustatius** provides a limited free-port influence: strategic importance and commercial power gained by permitting relatively open exchange among merchants operating across competing parent-realm systems.

The cleanest shorthand is **an autonomous Newport**: a prosperous port that developed its own charter government, harbor territory, dependencies, and direct overseas relationship instead of remaining one town within a larger province.

This comparison imports political and commercial structures, not the slave-trading economies associated with some historical precedents. Slavery remains outside Gauntlet's baseline labor system.

### Civic Government

The city is governed as a **merchant-dominated civic republic with real but limited elections**. It is neither a fully closed oligarchy nor a broadly democratic polity. Property-owning freemen elect representatives from the city's wards to a common council, while aldermen, major merchants, shipowners, financiers, leading tradesmen, and established civic families exercise disproportionate influence through wealth, patronage, credit, and durable political networks.

The mayor is chosen indirectly by the council from among the aldermen and serves a fixed term rather than ruling as a provincial governor. Under its charter, the civic corporation possesses unusually broad powers over the harbor, waterfront property, wharves, docks, ferries, markets, nearby islands, local courts, taxation, borrowing, public works, militia organization, harbor defenses, commercial regulation, and admission to civic privileges.

### Commercial Character

The city is unusually wealthy and influential for its territorial size, but its dependence on shipping, imported provisions, maritime credit, and access to neighboring markets makes diplomacy and commercial policy matters of survival as well as profit.

Its economy centers on entrepôt trade, warehousing, brokerage, customs, ship repair, shipping, finance, re-exported goods, tolerated smuggling, specialist crafts, nearby islands, and compact warmer-climate dependencies. Its permissive commercial culture allows merchants to maintain exchanges across competing political systems and can frustrate embargoes or restrictions imposed by neighboring governments.

### Religion and Toleration

The city is religiously pluralistic by settler standards. Its commercial history and civic autonomy make it more tolerant of differing branches of the shared faith and of private religious practice than the Inquisition stronghold. Exact limits remain unresolved; toleration should not be mistaken for modern secular equality.

### Defense

The city has the smallest total manpower reserve among the six major settler polities but the strongest single fortified harbor. Batteries, professional garrison troops, magazines, patrol vessels, privateers, repair yards, and defensible harbor approaches allow it to resist attack and disrupt regional sea lanes far beyond what its population would suggest.

Its weakness is lack of strategic depth. It cannot easily replace losses, sustain a large inland army, or endure a long siege without imported food, money, powder, and reinforcement.

### Relations with Other Polities and Native Governments

The city generally has fewer direct territorial ambitions than broad settler provinces and therefore often has practical incentives to preserve stable relations and recognized channels of negotiation. Its merchants nevertheless operate aggressively across jurisdictions.

Relations are not uniformly harmonious. Disputes may arise over fisheries, nearby islands, harbor access, pilotage, salvage, customs duties, debt, criminal jurisdiction, cargo seizures, privateering claims, and the conduct of individual traders. The city’s permissive commercial culture can complicate treaties made elsewhere by allowing merchants to continue trades that neighboring governments seek to restrict.

The city should conduct direct commercial and diplomatic relations with native governments where geography and trade make that useful. Those governments are political counterparts, not merely communities within a settler sphere.

Its precise charter language, detailed franchise qualifications, social hierarchy, denominational composition, limits of toleration, exact free-port and tariff rules, number and placement of batteries and forts, organization of militia and harbor patrols, surrounding dependencies, exact commercial agreements and jurisdictional disputes with neighboring polities and native governments, and later political development remain open for later development.

# PART XV — EXPLICIT GUARDRAILS

## 73. Do Not Reintroduce Without Discussion

The following directions have been rejected or superseded:

- **Heartlands** as a defined geographical or lore concept.
- The Gauntlet as a physical corridor, road, mountain pass, fortification, territory, institution, or legal ritual.
- “Breakthrough Victory” terminology unless separately adopted for rules reasons.
- An inciting collapse or single historical catastrophe as the necessary origin of all conflict.
- Six factions simply seeking supremacy after one central order collapsed.
- Factions as six monolithic organizations, nations, or uniform cultures.
- A medieval or late-medieval material baseline.
- Castles, keeps, plate-armored armies, pike warfare, ancient roads, ancient cities, lost empires, magical ruins, or inherited monumental architecture.
- A geographically empty “New World.”
- Native societies treated as a single culture, wilderness population, or obstacle to settlement.
- High-frequency, publicly accepted magic.
- The Alchemist as a generic wizard.
- The Spirit Walker as a generic or invented pan-native stereotype.
- Direct fantasy copies of Christianity, Puritanism, or other real religions.
- Detailed invented scriptures, prophets, cosmologies, and sacred histories added without need.
- Slavery as part of the setting’s baseline labor system.
- Faction colors dictating clothing.
- A uniformly gray, brown, grim, or lifeless visual world.
- Railroads, steam transport, industrial capitalism, or modern policing.
- Importing the creator’s personal dietary practices into the setting.

## 74. Development Discipline

When expanding the setting:

1. Work in small, concrete increments.
2. Separate confirmed decisions from speculative proposals.
3. Do not name places, peoples, faiths, wars, eras, or institutions prematurely.
4. Do not convert a useful ambiguity into fixed canon without discussion.
5. Preserve the setting’s vibrancy and human variety.
6. Prefer social, political, economic, and institutional conflict over a single world-defining catastrophe.
7. Keep supernatural elements consequential by keeping them uncommon, irregular, and resistant to normalization; allow rare events to matter historically.
8. Treat native nations as full political and cultural societies.
9. Ensure architecture and technology remain consistent with the eighteenth-century, pre-industrial baseline.
10. Remember that Gauntlet matches are abstractions, not literal maps of a single canonical war.

# PART XVII — CURRENT CANON SUMMARY

## 76. One-Page Reference

- **Era:** Eighteenth-century powder-and-parchment, pre-industrial, pre-rail.
- **Supernatural baseline:** Supernatural phenomena are real and uncommon but established in aggregate and institutionally consequential. Most people do not routinely witness unmistakable magic, reliable practitioners remain rare, and supernatural effects resist standardized reproduction or industrial use. Governments, churches, armies, scholars, financiers, diplomats, and intelligence networks nevertheless possess enough accumulated experience that serious supernatural claims cannot safely be ignored. Arcane practice can appear across factions; Mystics are the principal specialists rather than the sole users of supernatural methods. Rare supernatural events may alter wars, laws, settlements, diplomacy, commerce, and historical development without replacing ordinary material systems.
- **Reserved future-era emblem:** The six major settler polities eventually separate from their parent realms and unite as one nation, but the chronology, conflict, constitution, and political institutions of that later era remain outside the active setting timeline. The future nation’s principal civic emblem is a single solid, regular six-pointed colonial or heraldic star, with one point for each founding polity and the full star representing their union. It is not a hexagram or two interlocking triangles. The product logo may layer a stylized G over this star, but the G is not part of the in-world national emblem; the correspondence with the game’s six factions is an out-of-world design resonance.
- **Political order:** Six major settler polities—an old river province, maritime commonwealth, religious settlement, chartered territory, frontier province, and autonomous city—exist alongside smaller towns, districts, company posts, dependencies, private settlements, islands, military jurisdictions, and disputed communities. Independent native nations and confederations remain separate political powers. Exactly three major parent realms divide nominal authority over the six on a 3–2–1 basis: the dominant realm claims the old river province, maritime commonwealth, and religious settlement; its principal rival claims the chartered territory and frontier province; and the diminished third realm claims the autonomous city.
- **Parent realms:** The dominant realm is a crown-and-representative maritime imperial power with a strong navy, common-law political culture, mercantile trade system, and comparatively moderate established branch of the shared faith. Its principal rival is a more centralized, court-centered monarchy with a strong professional army, strategic forts, licensed inland trade, missions, and native alliances. The diminished third realm is an older territorial monarchy whose former crown-backed religious tribunal left lasting institutional scars and whose remaining power is concentrated in old fortified ports, strategic possessions, and residual diplomatic influence.
- **Present political moment:** The active present lies shortly after the recent continental war, amid new revenue demands, customs enforcement, retained troops, quartering grievances, western settlement restrictions, and early constitutional disputes. Resistance remains predominantly local, loyalist, reformist, and constitutional. Inter-polity coordination is tentative and builds on older ad hoc congress practices rather than a mature revolutionary movement.
- **Regional economy:** The six settler polities are economically specialized but mutually dependent. The old river province combines tobacco-led agriculture, grain, livestock, river commerce, landed wealth, and expanding shipping; the maritime commonwealth centers on overseas trade, shipbuilding, fishing, insurance, credit, printing, and small manufacturing; the religious settlement combines fisheries, timber, shipbuilding, metalworking, printing, and strong household and town production; the chartered territory channels timber, naval stores, furs, and inland trade through company warehouses and transport corridors; the frontier province is resource-rich but cash-poor, producing livestock, grain, timber, furs, land claims, and military supplies while relying on outside credit and transport; and the autonomous city thrives as an entrepôt through warehousing, brokerage, ship repair, customs, tolerated smuggling, islands, and warmer-climate dependencies.
- **Settler military balance:** No polity dominates every military domain. The old river province has the greatest manpower and conventional land-war potential; the maritime commonwealth has the strongest locally controlled maritime capacity; the religious settlement has the most cohesive militia and organized coastal defense; the chartered territory has the strongest professional logistics and fortified-corridor apparatus; the frontier province excels in Rangers, scouting, irregular warfare, and dispersed fort defense; and the autonomous city possesses the strongest single fortified harbor and concentrated garrison. Parent-realm regular forces remain distinct.
- **Regional settler identities:** The old river province is proud, hospitable, landed, status-conscious, politically ambitious, and protective of personal independence; the maritime commonwealth is practical, cosmopolitan, commercially minded, argumentative, tolerant, and fiercely municipal; the religious settlement is disciplined, literate, communal, morally serious, and reputation-conscious; the chartered territory is orderly, contractual, professionally competent, commercially mobile, and institutionally minded; the frontier province is self-reliant, improvisational, neighborly, suspicious of distant authority, and accustomed to danger; and the autonomous city is urbane, ceremonious, cosmopolitan, intensely civic-minded, and proud of its antiquity and privileges. These are tendencies and stereotypes, not universal traits.
- **Native political world:** Native political history long predates settler arrival. Three representative structures anchor current development: a decentralized coastal people composed of locally governed communities; a riverine paramountcy joining constituent peoples under a powerful but not absolute paramount leader; and a durable inland confederacy whose member nations retain internal sovereignty while participating in a Grand Council. They trade, intermarry, fight, ally, negotiate, and remember older histories independently of settler politics. No permanent pan-native government unites them.
- **Settler–native relations:** Treaties, kinship, trade, military cooperation, land disputes, competing jurisdiction, settlement pressure, missionary activity, and war all shape relations. Settler governments cannot assume that parent-realm charters extinguish native sovereignty. Native nations may cooperate with one settler polity against another without becoming clients or surrendering independent objectives.
- **Factions:** Military, Diplomats, Inquisition, Mystics, Intelligence, and Financiers are institutional spheres and methods of power rather than six nations or monolithic organizations. Their personnel and networks cross jurisdictional boundaries. Arcane practice is also cross-faction: Military may employ a War Witch, Diplomats may use divination, Financiers may experiment with actuarial alchemy, and Intelligence may pursue spectral surveillance without becoming Mystics.
- **Inquisition:** The Inquisition is an extreme and fundamentalist current within the wider shared faith. It is not identical to religion as a whole and is not recognized equally everywhere. It accepts that genuine anomalous phenomena occur but rejects “magic” as a neutral or legitimate category of practice, contesting what supernatural phenomena are, whether humans may manipulate them, and who has authority to judge them. Its own field practice therefore includes records, tests, countermeasures, and procedures derived from genuine encounters.
- **Mystics:** Mystics are the principal specialists in deliberate supernatural practice. The Alchemist approaches hidden forces through experiment, transformation, instruments, texts, and dangerous natural philosophy; the Spirit Walker works through inherited ceremonial traditions, spirits, place, obligation, death, identity, and ritual. Their methods overlap enough to share game mechanics, but their explanations of what they are doing need not agree.
- **Labor and society:** Chattel slavery is not part of the setting’s social or economic baseline. Labor instead includes family farming, tenancy, wages, seasonal and migrant work, apprenticeships, fixed-term contracted service, household service, debt dependency, and penal labor in harsher jurisdictions. Society remains unequal, status-conscious, patriarchal, and comparatively fluid rather than egalitarian or modern.
- **Religion:** Most settler polities share variations of one broad faith, but establishments, institutions, observance, tolerance, and political influence vary greatly. Ordinary religious life includes congregations, preaching, charity, education, family observance, public ceremonies, theological disagreement, nominal adherence, and sincere opposition to Inquisitorial excess. Native ceremonial traditions remain distinct and should not be reduced to settler religious categories.
- **Law and information:** Law is formal but fragmented, combining inherited common-law principles, local statutes and charters, municipal and company jurisdiction, native legal systems, treaty obligations, military justice, and Inquisition tribunals where recognized. Printing is widespread and partisan; postal and courier networks are regular enough to connect the continent but slow, vulnerable to interception, and politically important.
- **Medicine:** Ordinary medicine remains indispensable. Supernatural healing can occasionally achieve extraordinary results, but it is unreliable, dangerous, expensive, disputed in explanation, and incapable of replacing physicians, surgeons, apothecaries, midwives, traditional healers, quarantine, or public-health practice.
- **Warfare:** Warfare is early modern and logistically grounded: muskets, rifles, artillery, bayonets, forts, militias, regulars, Rangers, privateers, roads, rivers, magazines, wagons, and seasons matter. Rare supernatural involvement can alter a battle, siege, or campaign and sometimes leave major historical consequences, but armies cannot build strategy around dependable magical support.
- **Built environment:** There are no ancient cities, magical ruins, lost empires, castles, inherited monumental structures, railroads, or steam systems. Buildings and infrastructure belong to the current civilization and should feel built, maintained, expanded, and actively inhabited. Supernatural visual cues should usually appear as exceptional incidents, ritual traces, unusual objects, institutional precautions, or rare practitioners rather than omnipresent enchanted architecture.
- **Gauntlet itself:** The Gauntlet is not a literal geographical corridor or institution. A match is an abstraction of two powers struggling through escalating resistance using military, political, financial, covert, religious, diplomatic, and supernatural methods.
