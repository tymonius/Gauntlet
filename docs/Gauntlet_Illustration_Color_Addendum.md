# Gauntlet Illustration Color Addendum

**Status:** Active supplement to the [Gauntlet Illustration Art Direction](Gauntlet_Illustration_Art_Direction.md).  
**Purpose:** Ensure that historical grounding produces attractive, collectible card art rather than an unnecessarily drab or uniformly brown visual world.

This addendum governs color richness within Gauntlet illustrations. It does not change the setting period, faction-color rules, material realism, or restrained treatment of the supernatural.

---

## 1. Core color principle

> **Gauntlet uses rich painterly historical color, not bleak brown monotony.**

Historical realism should not be interpreted as a requirement that every image be dominated by mud, smoke, gray skies, brown coats, and weathered timber. Gauntlet is a card game whose artwork is a major part of its appeal. Its illustrations should be grounded, but also beautiful, memorable, and rewarding to examine.

The target is:

- historically plausible color;
- painterly richness;
- controlled saturation;
- strong focal contrast;
- intentional visual pleasure.

Avoid both extremes:

- **drab realism**, in which every scene collapses into brown and gray;
- **fantasy saturation**, in which neon color, magical glow, or arbitrary brightness overwhelms material credibility.

---

## 2. Practical color standard

Most illustrations should include:

1. a believable natural or architectural base palette;
2. one or two stronger color anchors;
3. enough value and hue contrast to remain appealing and legible at card size;
4. a memorable color relationship appropriate to the scene.

Useful sources of color include:

- blue, red, buff, and other period military cloth;
- autumn foliage, summer greenery, blue sky, water, and changing weather;
- white marble, pale plaster, brick red, painted clapboard, and dark polished wood;
- brass, copper, gold, glass, ceramic, and colored textiles;
- sealing wax, printed broadsides, maps, rugs, curtains, banners, and furnishings;
- candlelight, firelight, laboratory flame, sunrise, and sunset;
- botanical materials, ritual pigments, medicines, dyes, minerals, and specimens.

Color should arise naturally from the people, materials, light, season, and environment already present in the scene.

---

## 3. Faction colors remain graphic identifiers

This addendum does **not** make faction colors part of prescribed character or environmental design.

Faction colors continue to belong primarily to:

- card borders;
- faction sheets;
- icons and emblems;
- trackers;
- interface elements;
- other player-facing graphic systems.

Illustrations should gain color from their own subject matter rather than forcing crimson, royal blue, green, violet, gold, or charcoal into every scene associated with a faction.

---

## 4. Scene-specific color opportunities

### Military

Use uniform cloth, officer sashes, brass artillery, green terrain, blue sky, autumn foliage, powder smoke, camp equipment, and standards to prevent battle scenes from becoming uniformly brown.

### Diplomats and civic power

These scenes may be among the most visually luminous and color-rich through white marble, polished wood, blue or red drapery, wax seals, formal clothing, carpets, painted interiors, and strong daylight.

### Financiers

Use dark green or burgundy textiles when natural, walnut, brass, ivory paper, red wax, painted merchant signs, harbor blues, cargo, imported goods, and warm counting-house interiors.

### Intelligence

Subtle scenes can still use rich urban interiors, printed material, maps, candlelight, garden or street color, clothing variation, and strong distinctions between respectable surfaces and concealed activity.

### Mystics

Avoid default neon magic. Draw color instead from glass, liquids, minerals, flame, plants, ritual paint, feathers, woven textiles, smoke, reflections, and unusual but material light effects.

### Inquisition

The Inquisition may remain the most restrained faction visually, but it should not become colorless. Use strong black-and-white contrast, candle gold, ochre wax, red seals, pale skin, ash gray, dark wood, aged paper, and controlled firelight.

---

## 5. Production guardrails

Do:

- use concentrated color to direct attention;
- allow sunlight, interiors, clothing, vegetation, and objects to create beauty;
- vary palettes across the card pool;
- preserve material texture and believable light;
- consider the artwork as a collectible image, not merely a documentary reconstruction.

Do not:

- add random bright objects solely to increase saturation;
- color-code characters by faction;
- use constant purple, blue, or green magical glow;
- make every battlefield overcast and muddy;
- make every interior dark brown;
- flatten the entire card pool into one muted palette;
- imitate modern cinematic teal-and-orange grading as a universal look.

---

## 6. Approval question

Add this question to illustration review:

> **Does the image contain a memorable and appropriate use of color, or has historical grounding been mistaken for drabness?**

An approved Gauntlet illustration should feel materially credible and visually inviting at the same time.
