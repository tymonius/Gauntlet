# Gauntlet Illustration Art Direction

**Status:** Active production-facing visual-world reference.  
**Purpose:** Define the shared setting, architecture, clothing, technology, environmental, and illustration standards for Gauntlet artwork.

This document governs the world depicted inside illustrations. The [Visual Design Language](Gauntlet_Visual_Design_Language.md) governs cards, borders, typography, faction colors, emblems, interfaces, and other graphic systems. The [Leader Design Bible](Gauntlet_v0.6_Leader_Design_Bible.md) governs the specific silhouette, props, pose, and miniature direction for each Leader.

When older general art guidance conflicts with this document, this document governs shared world and illustration direction. Specific approved Leader designs remain governed by the Leader Design Bible.

---

## 1. Core visual statement

> **Gauntlet is a powder-and-parchment fantasy world grounded confidently in colonial and early American architecture, clothing, warfare, civic design, and material culture. Its institutions pursue power through battle, negotiation, finance, doctrine, secrecy, and supernatural practice.**

The setting should look historically plausible at first glance and reveal its fictional institutions and supernatural undercurrent through the event being depicted.

The setting does not need arbitrary architectural distortions, invented vegetation, unfamiliar construction, or decorative oddities merely to signal that it is fictional. Avoid the literal American flag and other direct United States national symbols; otherwise, use colonial and early American design language without apology.

The fiction should come from Gauntlet's own:

- institutions and conflicts;
- characters and professions;
- political structure;
- invented civic, military, company, and religious identities;
- restrained supernatural phenomena.

---

## 2. Governing visual principles

### 2.1 Historical grounding

The default visual and technological center is the **mid-to-late eighteenth century**, with selective early nineteenth-century developments where appropriate.

Primary references include:

- colonial and early American towns, forts, ports, farms, and civic centers;
- British regular, Continental Army, militia, minuteman, frontier-company, artillery, and dragoon visual language;
- neoclassical civic architecture;
- early laboratories, counting houses, printing shops, warehouses, shipyards, and manufactories;
- flintlock warfare, animal transport, sailing vessels, physical correspondence, and hand-kept records.

Historical references supply material culture and visual grammar, not literal national history.

### 2.2 The Gauntlet is not a physical place

The Gauntlet is the game's metaphor for overcoming the full sequence of an opponent's resistance. It is not a universal road, pass, corridor, mountain feature, ancient causeway, or named region in the setting.

Artwork should not attempt to explain the game by depicting:

- six literal card-shaped locations;
- a single narrow world-road called the Gauntlet;
- a chain of strongpoints that every match represents;
- the Last Stand as a geographical place.

Illustrations may depict roads, passes, bridges, forts, towns, and contested approaches when a card or scene calls for them, but those places are ordinary parts of the world rather than a literalized board.

### 2.3 Factions are institutions, not cultures

The six factions represent methods and institutions of power. They are not six nations, ethnic groups, uniformed orders, or isolated civilizations.

Characters in the same faction may differ widely in:

- culture and geographic background;
- profession;
- social class;
- clothing;
- environment;
- tools and equipment;
- visual silhouette.

The Ranger is a frontiersman while the Spymaster is a city-dwelling elite with a secret identity. The Spirit Walker is a native ritual specialist with a ceremonial headdress while the Alchemist wears an early laboratory coat. Their common faction reflects what kind of power they wield, not a shared wardrobe.

### 2.4 Faction colors are graphic, not diegetic

Faction colors primarily identify player-facing game components:

- card borders;
- faction sheets;
- headings;
- trackers;
- icons;
- interface elements;
- print and digital references.

They are not prescribed wardrobe, heraldry, architecture, lighting, or environmental palettes. A character may naturally wear a faction-associated color when it suits the design, but no illustration should force that color into the scene solely to announce faction membership.

### 2.5 Institutional identity through action

A faction should usually be legible through:

- what its people are doing;
- the power they exercise;
- the environment in which they work;
- their tools, records, personnel, and systems;
- the consequences of their actions.

The surrounding card frame already identifies the faction. The artwork should tell the story.

## 3. Illustration style

### 3.1 Painterly historical realism

Gauntlet artwork should use **painterly historical realism**:

- believable anatomy, architecture, clothing, equipment, and materials;
- compositions with the intent and drama of historical painting;
- visible texture rather than glossy photographic perfection;
- selective detail and atmospheric depth;
- restrained stylization that preserves card-size readability.

The ideal impression is:

> **An eighteenth-century historical painting depicting events from a world in which Gauntlet's institutions and supernatural forces are real.**

Avoid generic digital-fantasy concept art, glossy costume photography, cinematic medieval-war posters, steampunk illustration, and heavily airbrushed imagery.

### 3.2 Material seriousness

Favor tactile depictions of:

- wool and linen;
- leather;
- timber and brick;
- pale stone and white marble;
- parchment, paper, wax, and ink;
- blackened iron, brass, copper, and glass;
- earth, mud, smoke, water, and weather.

Buildings, uniforms, documents, weapons, and equipment should look handled, transported, maintained, repaired, stained, and used.

### 3.3 Lighting

Use light sources that belong in the scene:

- daylight and overcast sky;
- dawn and sunset;
- candles, fireplaces, and lanterns;
- forge and laboratory burners;
- muzzle flashes and cannon fire.

Avoid colored magical lighting as the default source of drama.

### 3.4 Composition

Every illustration should answer three questions clearly:

1. What is happening?
2. Who currently has the advantage?
3. What decision or consequence defines the moment?

Prefer:

- one primary conflict;
- one to three major figures for ordinary card art;
- strong silhouettes;
- a clear relationship between people and environment;
- enough background detail to establish the world without competing with the focal event.

Avoid assembling all six factions into a single scene merely to advertise the setting.

### 3.5 Scale

Most art should remain at a human or local scale:

- a chamber where policy is decided;
- a road where a column is delayed;
- a bridge being contested;
- a warehouse being sealed;
- a laboratory result being observed;
- a witness being questioned;
- a fort preparing for attack.

Reserve panoramic army and city scenes for promotional art, faction splashes, and especially consequential cards.

### 3.6 No added text

Illustrations should contain no added title, faction name, caption, logo, card frame, or decorative typography. In-world documents and signs may contain incidental marks or writing when natural to the scene, but the image itself should never label the card.

## 4. Architecture and settlements

### 4.1 Everyday built environment

The default built environment should lean strongly toward colonial and early American architecture:

- clapboard houses and shops;
- red-brick civic and commercial buildings;
- stone foundations and large masonry chimneys;
- shingled roofs, dormers, shutters, cupolas, and modest steeples;
- meetinghouses, taverns, counting houses, workshops, mills, warehouses, and wharves;
- fenced lots, town greens, muddy streets, wooden bridges, and wagon yards;
- timber stockades, blockhouses, earthworks, and small artillery forts.

Do not add unfamiliar features merely to distance the setting from real America. Avoid the literal American flag and direct national symbols; otherwise, let the architecture read clearly.

### 4.2 Settlement scales

#### Provincial capital

A developed center of government, finance, law, and diplomacy:

- neoclassical capitol, assembly, court, treasury, or executive buildings;
- central square or formal civic grounds;
- banks, printers, offices, townhouses, and major taverns;
- organized militia and administrative presence;
- riverfront, harbor, or important road connection.

#### Market town

The common setting for public action, unrest, trade, recruitment, and intrigue:

- mixed brick and clapboard construction;
- tavern, merchant, smithy, stable, printer, small meeting hall, and market green;
- wagons, livestock, notices, drills, travelers, and passing troops;
- prosperity and authority that remain visibly vulnerable.

#### Frontier settlement or fort

The edge of reliable institutional control:

- palisades, blockhouses, ditches, earthworks, and artillery positions;
- rough barracks, storehouses, tents, wagons, workshops, and timber bridges;
- civilian buildings growing around a military or commercial post;
- scouts, militia, laborers, traders, soldiers, and displaced civilians sharing the space.

### 4.3 Secular political power

When architecture must communicate governmental, legislative, judicial, administrative, or elite secular authority, prefer:

- white marble or pale stone;
- neoclassical facades;
- columns and porticos;
- domes and cupolas;
- broad staircases;
- pediments and entablatures;
- formal symmetry and monumental civic geometry.

This replaces the default fantasy shorthand of castles, palaces, keeps, and throne rooms.

Neoclassical authority is especially appropriate for:

- assemblies and senates;
- secular courts;
- executive halls;
- diplomatic buildings;
- treasuries and major banks;
- archives and public institutions.

### 4.4 Inquisition architecture

The Inquisition does **not** use the secular neoclassical language as its default.

Its architecture should communicate austerity, enclosure, age, scrutiny, and doctrinal authority through:

- dark brick, rough stone, heavy timber, and limewashed masonry;
- severe meetinghouses, fortified chapels, tribunal halls, archives, cloisters, and secluded compounds;
- steep roofs, narrow windows, bell towers, enclosed courtyards, iron gates, and restrained ornament;
- long tables, shelves of testimony, reliquaries, locked cabinets, and candlelit corridors.

The building should make visitors feel watched and judged before proceedings begin.

### 4.5 Architectural exclusions

Avoid as default setting language:

- medieval castles and curtain walls;
- Gothic cathedrals dominating every town;
- baroque royal palaces;
- Roman coliseums;
- Renaissance city-states;
- fantasy towers and exaggerated rooflines;
- steampunk pipes, gears, and machinery.

Old structures may exist where a specific scene requires them, but current institutions should visibly adapt them to eighteenth-century needs.

## 5. Interior language

### 5.1 Secular civic and political interiors

Use:

- pale stone or plaster;
- columns or pilasters;
- large windows and high ceilings;
- polished wood floors and marble fireplaces;
- symmetry, public galleries, benches, lecterns, dais, or judicial bench;
- maps, statutes, seals, registers, and proclamations.

Mood: **legitimacy, law, public authority, reason, procedure.**

### 5.2 Financial interiors

Use:

- dark polished wood and brass fixtures;
- gridded windows;
- ledgers, manifests, scales, bonds, keys, seals, and locked drawers;
- orderly desks and clerical work;
- wall maps of supply, trade, property, and transportation;
- strongboxes, vaults, and filing systems.

Mood: **control, leverage, confidence, infrastructure, quiet power.**

### 5.3 Military interiors

Use:

- campaign tables and pinned maps;
- dispatch boxes;
- practical furniture;
- muskets, sabers, pistols, powder, tools, and supply shelves;
- drums, standards, field equipment, and signs of active work.

Mood: **readiness, discipline, urgency, logistics.**

### 5.4 Inquisition interiors

Use:

- dark masonry and heavy timber;
- narrow windows and candlelight;
- long tables, witness stools, confession benches, and document racks;
- bound testimony, sealed records, reliquaries, locks, keys, iron hardware, and enclosed spaces.

Mood: **judgment, austerity, surveillance, moral pressure.**

Avoid reducing every Inquisition interior to a torture chamber.

### 5.5 Mystic interiors

Mystic spaces are usually ordinary rooms adapted to unusual work.

Alchemist spaces may include:

- early laboratory coat, apron, gloves, notebooks, and formulae;
- retorts, alembics, crucibles, burners, balances, vials, glassware, and brass instruments;
- stains, scorch marks, specimens, and incomplete experiments.

Spirit Walker spaces may include:

- ceremonial headdress and culturally coherent ritual dress;
- woven textiles, hide, feathers, bone, wood, shell, natural pigments, and inherited ritual objects;
- drums, rattles, staffs, bowls, bundles, smoke vessels, medicines, and prepared materials.

Mood: **disciplined practice, inherited knowledge, curiosity, danger, altered perception.**

### 5.6 Intelligence interiors

Use spaces that appear socially ordinary before their hidden purpose becomes visible:

- respectable offices, private homes, tavern rooms, printers, safe houses, and observation posts;
- concealed drawers, duplicate papers, coded notes, maps, false seals, trunks, and hidden compartments;
- mundane furnishings with multiple routes in and out.

Mood: **secrecy, ambiguity, pressure, preparation.**

## 6. Landscape and terrain

### 6.1 Default landscape

Draw primarily from eastern and northeastern American environments:

- wooded ridges and rolling farmland;
- river valleys, streams, marshes, fords, and bridges;
- dense forest broken by roads, fields, and settlements;
- low mountains, rocky clearings, bluffs, and defensible rises;
- muddy roads, fenced pasture, mills, depots, farms, and small ports.

The landscape should feel partially settled, strategically legible, and under pressure from weather, distance, transport, and conflict.

### 6.2 Terrain character

Terrain should suggest:

- lines of advance;
- chokepoints;
- overwatch positions;
- cover and exposure;
- approach routes;
- centers of control;
- fallback positions.

Even when not illustrating gameplay literally, the land should make tactical decisions visible.

### 6.3 Roads and crossings

Roads are power made visible. Use:

- packed dirt and muddy military roads;
- rutted wagon tracks;
- corduroy roads over soft ground;
- raised roads through wetlands;
- maintained roads controlled through taxation, guards, or supply;
- neglected roads becoming logistical hazards.

Crossings may include timber bridges, stone arches in developed areas, ferries, fords, causeways, and fortified approaches.

### 6.4 High ground

High ground may be a wooded ridge, cleared rise, river bluff, hill crest, battery position, or slope above a road. It does not need to be a dramatic fantasy cliff.

### 6.5 Weather and season

Strong recurring conditions include:

- morning mist;
- rain-darkened roads;
- autumn haze;
- winter bare branches;
- woodsmoke and cannon smoke;
- hard afternoon light;
- overcast skies;
- evening lamplight.

Late summer, autumn, early spring, and cold but not fully frozen winter fit the setting especially well.

Avoid making every scene stormy, moonlit, blue-gray, or apocalyptic.

### 6.6 Supernatural landscapes

The landscape should remain landscape first. Prefer:

- birds falling silent in one grove;
- a road remaining dry in rain;
- frost forming in a small patch of field;
- a pond reflecting the wrong sky;
- smoke drifting toward one person;
- sound carrying impossibly across a ridge;
- animals reacting before people notice anything.

Avoid floating rocks, giant rune fields, glowing craters, and decorative energy effects.

## 7. Clothing and character design

### 7.1 Design order

Design each character in this order:

1. culture and geographic background;
2. profession and daily function;
3. social status;
4. individual personality;
5. historical archetype;
6. faction only as expressed through the kind of power being exercised.

Faction membership does not prescribe clothing.

### 7.2 Civilian clothing

Appropriate men's clothing includes:

- linen shirts;
- waistcoats;
- knee breeches or early trousers;
- stockings and buckled shoes;
- frock coats, working coats, hunting shirts, and travel coats;
- cocked hats, broad-brimmed hats, felt hats, knit caps, and practical headwear;
- neckcloths, cravats, and scarves.

Appropriate women's clothing includes:

- fitted jackets or bodices over skirts;
- colonial gowns with restrained silhouettes;
- caps, kerchiefs, shawls, cloaks, and aprons;
- riding habits and tailored coats for travel or professional roles;
- sturdy shoes and boots where appropriate.

Wealth should read through quality, fit, cleanliness, materials, and subtle ornament rather than theatrical excess.

### 7.3 Leader individuality

Leaders within one faction may look radically different because they embody different professions and expressions of power.

Examples:

- the General is a mounted field commander; the Commandant is a severe garrison administrator;
- the Ambassador is a personal negotiator; the Senator is a formal civic orator;
- the Ranger is a frontier rifleman and surveyor; the Spymaster is a composed urban elite operating behind a respectable identity;
- the Alchemist wears an early lab coat; the Spirit Walker wears native ceremonial dress and a tribal headdress;
- the Grand Inquisitor wears formal religious-judicial dress; the Witch Hunter wears practical field gear;
- the Banker is an urban professional; the Executive is an operational merchant-industrialist or chartered-company leader.

Do not redesign leader pairs as coordinated costumes.

### 7.4 Military clothing

Military design should lean toward:

- British redcoat and Continental Army regulars;
- colonial militia and minutemen;
- frontier companies;
- artillery crews;
- dragoons and mounted officers.

Typical soldiers use:

- regimental coats or mixed civilian coats;
- waistcoats and breeches;
- gaiters or boots;
- cocked hats, caps, or practical civilian hats;
- crossbelts, cartridge boxes, canteens, packs, blankets, and campaign equipment;
- flintlock muskets and bayonets.

Militia and minutemen should show mixed civilian clothing, personal arms, uneven equipment, and limited visual uniformity.

Officers may carry sabers, pistols, command batons, maps, dispatch cases, and spyglasses.

Move away from:

- pikes and pike formations;
- breastplates and transitional armor;
- medieval helmets and shields;
- sword-heavy infantry;
- any visual midpoint between knights and musketeers.

### 7.5 Costume exclusions

Avoid:

- faction-color uniforms as universal shorthand;
- full medieval plate;
- fantasy leather armor covered in straps;
- oversized pauldrons;
- hooded cloaks on every covert character;
- Victorian suits;
- steampunk goggles;
- pirate costume as generic frontier dress;
- excessive use of powdered wigs or tricorns on everyone;
- unexplained ceremonial ornament on ordinary characters.

## 8. Weapons, equipment, and technology

### 8.1 Technology baseline

The setting supports:

- flintlock firearms;
- field artillery;
- organized military logistics;
- sailing ships and river craft;
- printing presses and widespread pamphlets;
- surveying, cartography, optics, clocks, and precision tools;
- early scientific laboratories;
- water-powered mills, workshops, foundries, shipyards, and limited mechanized production.

Power still depends heavily on people, animals, paper, roads, ships, labor, and weather.

### 8.2 Firearms

The standard infantry weapon is the flintlock musket with bayonet. Rifles belong mainly to Rangers, hunters, scouts, marksmen, and specialist light infantry. Pistols are common among officers, cavalry, travelers, Witch Hunters, spies, guards, and wealthy civilians.

Battles should read as volleys, powder smoke, formations, bayonet pressure, skirmishing, artillery, and broken lines—not masses of swordsmen.

### 8.3 Edged weapons

Sabers, hangers, smallswords, hunting swords, and cutlasses remain appropriate for officers, cavalry, duelists, naval personnel, and specialized fighters. Practical knives, hatchets, tomahawks, axes, and bayonets belong to work and field use.

Swords should not replace firearms as the normal battlefield weapon.

### 8.4 Artillery and fortification

Appropriate artillery includes field guns, garrison cannon, mortars, howitzers, naval guns, and small swivel guns.

Show the supporting system:

- limbers and ammunition wagons;
- ramrods, sponges, worms, handspikes, and drag ropes;
- powder chests, shot, surveying instruments, and trained crews.

Appropriate fortifications include palisades, blockhouses, ditches, earthen ramparts, redoubts, bastions, batteries, abatis, fascines, gabions, and fortified crossings.

### 8.5 Logistics and transportation

Recurring equipment includes:

- wagons, carts, packhorses, draft horses, and oxen;
- barrels, sacks, crates, ration boxes, tents, portable forges, medical chests, and ammunition wagons;
- coaches, courier horses, ferries, flatboats, barges, merchant ships, schooners, brigs, and frigates.

Armies should not appear to materialize without supplies, animals, laborers, roads, and camp infrastructure.

### 8.6 Communication and paper

Communication remains physical:

- handwritten letters;
- coded dispatches;
- printed broadsides, newspapers, and pamphlets;
- messengers;
- signal flags, drums, horns, and beacons;
- seals, passports, warrants, contracts, bonds, testimony, and registers.

There is no ordinary telegraph. Delay, interception, forgery, leakage, and outdated information are important parts of the setting.

### 8.7 Industry and science

The setting supports proto-industrial activity:

- sawmills, gristmills, textile workshops, powder mills, glassworks, ropewalks, armories, printing houses, foundries, and small manufactories;
- human, animal, water, treadle, and hand-cranked power;
- gears, belts, pulleys, and tools used for plausible work rather than decorative steampunk spectacle.

Scientific instruments may include microscopes, telescopes, balances, thermometers, barometers, chronometers, lenses, anatomical diagrams, specimen boxes, and rare experimental electrical apparatus such as Leyden jars.

### 8.8 Technology ceiling

Do not use as ordinary background technology:

- steam locomotives or steamships;
- telegraphs;
- modern factories;
- revolvers, repeating rifles, or cartridge firearms;
- dynamite or modern explosives;
- electrical infrastructure;
- elaborate automatons;
- standardized magical machinery;
- magical rifles, energy artillery, or flying war machines.

A useful test is whether an object could plausibly appear in an unusually advanced workshop around 1800. Unique dangerous prototypes may exist, but should not quietly become the setting's baseline.

## 9. Faction depiction language

Faction identity should come from action and institutional context rather than costume color.

### 9.1 Military

Show:

- formations responding to orders;
- militia becoming organized;
- artillery positions and fortified approaches;
- roads, supply columns, engineers, messengers, reserves, and command posts;
- ground being taken, held, or prepared.

Leader contrast: the General advances and inspires; the Commandant disciplines, fortifies, and holds.

### 9.2 Diplomats

Show:

- treaties negotiated, signed, delayed, rejected, or exploited;
- recognition, witnesses, ceremony, concession, and procedural legitimacy;
- civilians constraining military action through documents and public authority;
- private hostility beneath formal civility.

Leader contrast: the Ambassador works through personal negotiation and backchannels; the Senator works through law, rhetoric, procedure, and institutional legitimacy.

### 9.3 Financiers

Show:

- credit becoming supplies, labor, ships, construction, and military capacity;
- debt and ownership changing political decisions;
- ledgers coordinating distant activity;
- clerks, warehouses, ports, contracts, keys, seals, bonds, and manifests controlling material systems.

Leader contrast: the Banker controls abstract value, credit, and leverage; the Executive controls operations, acquisition, production, and logistics.

### 9.4 Intelligence

Show:

- useful information reaching one side first;
- false information reaching the other;
- observation, intercepted correspondence, forged documents, covert networks, disguised identities, and manipulated decisions;
- hidden information visible to the viewer but not to the people in the scene.

Leader contrast: the Ranger disappears into terrain; the Spymaster disappears into society.

### 9.5 Mystics

Show:

- disciplined experiment, ritual, interpretation, transformation, or communion;
- phenomena others may deny, misread, fear, or attempt to control;
- instruments responding to the unseen;
- matter, reflection, smoke, weather, memory, sound, or animal behavior acting impossibly but subtly.

Leader contrast: the Alchemist studies and manipulates matter; the Spirit Walker practices an inherited native ritual tradition and mediates with unseen forces.

### 9.6 Inquisition

Show:

- accusation becoming official truth;
- testimony collected, records sealed, objects examined or destroyed, doctrine enforced, and public interpretation controlled;
- field evidence brought before an austere institution;
- officials privately confronting evidence they publicly deny;
- Witch Hunters directly encountering supernatural phenomena while remaining zealously opposed to magic and magic users.

Leader contrast: the Grand Inquisitor judges through institutional authority; the Witch Hunter investigates, pursues, exposes, and destroys in the field.

### 9.7 Mixed-faction scenes

When factions share a scene, show competing interests rather than a lineup of representatives. Examples:

- a Military officer demands supplies from a Financier's warehouse;
- a Senator questions an Inquisition official in a public chamber;
- an Ambassador quietly acts on information supplied by the Spymaster;
- an Alchemist presents evidence that a Witch Hunter interprets as corruption;
- a Ranger's report contradicts the map used by the General.

## 10. Supernatural depiction

### 10.1 Real but disputable

The supernatural is real, but most events should leave room for denial, reinterpretation, doctrine, fear, or competing explanation.

Prefer evidence and consequence:

- smoke moving against the wind;
- a reflection showing something absent;
- a compass turning toward a person;
- frost around one sealed document;
- an impossible shadow;
- unusual light trapped in glass or liquid;
- animals reacting before witnesses;
- sound, memory, or weather behaving incorrectly.

Avoid giant rune circles, portals, hovering rocks, constant purple smoke, and decorative magical fireworks.

### 10.2 Magic does not replace infrastructure

Armies still require food, powder, horses, roads, and messages. Governments still require witnesses, records, and enforcement. Banks still require confidence and accounting. Mystical practice should not make ordinary institutions or technology irrelevant.

### 10.3 Character response

Reactions should reflect institutional worldview:

- the Alchemist observes and tests;
- the Spirit Walker recognizes and participates;
- the Witch Hunter condemns and destroys;
- the Grand Inquisitor controls the official interpretation;
- ordinary witnesses may fear, rationalize, deny, or misunderstand.

## 11. Card-art production standards

### 11.1 Card-size readability

Most card illustrations should use:

- one to three major figures;
- distinct silhouettes;
- clear foreground action;
- uncluttered focal areas;
- meaningful but subordinate background detail;
- strong separation between subject and environment.

Avoid placing the decisive story only in the distant background.

### 11.2 Crop safety

Compose with enough space to support card windows, responsive website crops, faction sheets, and promotional layouts.

Preserve:

- the full head, including hair and headwear;
- hands holding signature objects;
- weapon or instrument tips that establish silhouette;
- the General's horse and mounted read.

Excess cropping should come primarily from below the subject rather than above the head.

### 11.3 Action cards

Depict the decisive moment or consequence, not merely the card's noun.

Examples:

- **Embargo:** officials sealing a merchant warehouse while cargo remains idle;
- **Assassins:** danger already penetrating a respectable elite interior;
- **Armistice:** opposing forces halted as a formal proposal arrives;
- **Attrition:** exhausted troops and depleted material after repeated engagements;
- **Sabotage:** ordinary equipment failing at the critical moment.

### 11.4 Assets

Show enduring systems, positions, or capabilities:

- a working intelligence network;
- an established artillery battery;
- a printing operation;
- a functioning supply depot;
- a bank coordinating credit;
- a laboratory prepared for continued experimentation.

### 11.5 Conditions

Show a state affecting the broader situation:

- roads closed by official order;
- troops immobilized by policy, weather, or supply;
- negotiations creating a temporary pause;
- suspicion changing public behavior;
- a settlement or army operating under an ongoing constraint.

### 11.6 Territories

A Territory should look worth fighting over and communicate:

- strategic value;
- approach routes;
- defensible or exploitable features;
- human use and activity;
- a clear environmental identity.

Include people, structures, tracks, smoke, wagons, boats, signals, cultivation, or other evidence of use when appropriate. Pure landscapes should not make a strategic location feel inert.

---

## 12. Hard exclusions

Do not introduce as default Gauntlet imagery:

- literal card-shaped landscapes;
- a physical world feature called the Gauntlet;
- full-plate knights or medieval armies;
- pike formations and armored infantry;
- castles as the normal seat of power;
- Roman-style arenas;
- recognizable United States flags or national symbols;
- faction colors treated as mandatory costume colors;
- matching costumes for unrelated leaders in one faction;
- Gothic fantasy as the universal tone;
- steampunk machinery, goggles, or decorative gears;
- Victorian industrial cities;
- ubiquitous glowing magic;
- invented faction crests inserted into every scene;
- all six factions forced into every major illustration;
- embedded titles, labels, logos, or captions.

---

## 13. Approval checklist

Before approving an illustration, verify:

- Does it clearly belong to the colonial / early American visual period?
- Are architecture, clothing, firearms, tools, and technology appropriate?
- Have medieval knights, armor, pikes, castles, or sword-heavy armies crept in?
- Does profession, culture, status, and personality determine character design more than faction membership?
- Is faction identity communicated through action and context rather than wardrobe color?
- Is there one readable focal event?
- Does the image work at card size?
- Is the supernatural restrained, material, and purposeful?
- Are faces, hands, weapons, instruments, and structures credible?
- Is there unnecessary text or symbolism?
- Does the scene feel like an event in Gauntlet's world rather than a generic fantasy tableau?

---

## 14. Initial production sequence

The first art batch should test the visual system across environments and factions before returning to another comprehensive hero image:

1. **High Ground** — regulars, militia, or artillery establishing advantage on a wooded ridge.
2. **Supply Depot** — a fortified wagon yard and magazine under pressure.
3. **Embargo** — officials sealing a merchant warehouse at a river port.
4. **Assassins** — covert danger inside respectable urban society.
5. **Alchemist study** — early laboratory practice and restrained supernatural evidence.
6. **Inquisition tribunal** — the faction's separate austere architecture and institutional authority.

These studies should establish the recurring world language before another promotional image attempts to represent Gauntlet as a whole.
