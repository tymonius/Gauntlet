# Gauntlet Illustration Environmental Detail Guardrails

**Status:** Active supplement to the [Gauntlet Illustration Art Direction](Gauntlet_Illustration_Art_Direction.md).  
**Purpose:** Establish production rules for flags, signage, visible writing, environmental labeling, scene density, and card-size focal clarity.

These rules apply to all new Gauntlet illustrations and should be treated as carry-forward requirements during prompt writing, art review, revision, and production approval.

---

## 1. Flags and national symbols

- Do **not** use literal United States flags or other direct United States national symbols.
- Do not reproduce recognizable historical American flag designs with altered colors or only minor changes.
- Flags may appear where appropriate, but they should represent fictional armies, governments, towns, companies, ships, civic bodies, or institutions within Gauntlet.
- Do not add a large flag merely to fill open sky or identify the scene's allegiance.

Faction colors and emblems remain part of the player-facing graphic system. They are not required as in-world flags, banners, heraldry, or environmental symbols.

---

## 2. Signage and explanatory labels

- Do **not** add oversized explanatory labels to buildings, crates, barrels, wagons, equipment, or infrastructure merely to tell the viewer what an object is.
- Do **not** use labels such as `RATIONS FOR TROOPS`, `SUPPLIES`, `POWDER`, or similar blunt descriptions as compositional shortcuts unless a specific historically plausible context genuinely requires the wording.
- Do **not** use modern-style warning or safety signage such as `NO SMOKING`, `NO OPEN FLAME`, `KEEP OUT`, or comparable contemporary posted-notice language.
- Do not rely on text to explain the function of a depot, magazine, bank, laboratory, tribunal, warehouse, or other location. The scene's architecture, objects, personnel, and activity should communicate its purpose.

A powder magazine should read as a powder magazine because of its construction, guarded access, powder barrels, careful handling, isolation, and surrounding activity—not because a large sign labels it.

---

## 3. Historically plausible visible writing

Visible writing may appear when it naturally belongs in the scene, including:

- documents, warrants, contracts, ledgers, manifests, maps, proclamations, broadsides, and correspondence;
- small ownership marks, shipping marks, maker's marks, regimental designations, warehouse numbers, or inventory notation;
- restrained shop, tavern, office, or civic signage appropriate to the place and period;
- seals, stamps, handwritten notes, inscriptions, or painted identifiers that serve an actual in-world function.

Any visible writing should be:

- historically plausible;
- incidental rather than explanatory;
- subordinate to the main image;
- appropriately scaled and placed;
- unnecessary for understanding the card's central action.

Partial, weathered, distant, obscured, or only partly legible writing is often preferable to large clean text aimed directly at the viewer.

---

## 4. Environmental storytelling over labels

Communicate location and function through visual evidence.

Prefer:

- guarded doors and careful handling for a powder magazine;
- wagons, stores, quartermasters, laborers, and distribution activity for a supply depot;
- clerks, ledgers, scales, keys, bonds, and customers for a bank or counting house;
- glassware, burners, stains, notes, specimens, and active experimentation for an Alchemist's laboratory;
- witness seating, long tables, testimony shelves, sealed records, and controlled access for an Inquisition tribunal;
- concealed papers, duplicate seals, unusual sightlines, and discreet exchanges for an Intelligence location.

The viewer should understand the place by seeing how it operates.

---

## 5. Scene density and focal clarity

Gauntlet may depict busy forts, depots, ports, assemblies, battlefields, markets, and institutional interiors. Complexity is welcome when the location demands it, but complexity must remain organized.

Busy environments must preserve:

- one clearly dominant subject, action, or decision;
- a readable path for the eye toward the focal event;
- strong separation between important figures and background activity;
- subordinate secondary actions;
- sufficient value, hue, scale, or edge contrast to survive reduction to card size.

Avoid:

- crowds of equally emphasized figures;
- many unrelated actions competing for attention;
- uniform detail and sharpness across the entire image;
- foreground clutter that blocks the main actors;
- distant focal events that disappear at card size.

A scene may contain extensive logistical or social activity while still making it immediately clear what the card is about.

---

## 6. Approval checklist

Before approving an illustration, verify:

- Are all flags and symbols fictional and free of recognizable United States national imagery?
- Has any flag been added merely as an easy allegiance marker or compositional filler?
- Are signs, labels, and visible writing historically plausible and appropriately scaled?
- Is any text explaining an object or location that the visual storytelling should explain instead?
- Has modern warning, safety, or instructional signage appeared?
- Would the scene remain understandable if every incidental word were illegible?
- In a busy scene, is there still one clear focal path and primary action at card size?
- Are secondary details enriching the scene rather than competing with it?

If any answer reveals a problem, revise the illustration before approval.
