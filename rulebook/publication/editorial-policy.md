# Rules Publication Editorial Policy

This policy applies to the Player's Guide, Faction Guides, Comprehensive Gauntlet Rules, Reference Cards, and the Rules Arbiter's player-facing answers.

## Defined terms must still read as normal English

Defined game terms name game concepts. They do not replace natural grammar.

Write the sentence as ordinary English first, then use the defined term where it naturally belongs. Capitalization or formal definition is never a reason to use stilted, code-like, or noun-heavy phrasing.

Prefer language that describes what the player actually does.

Good examples:

- force your opponent into a **Last Stand**;
- defeat your opponent in the resulting **battle**;
- advance your **Front Line**;
- occupy an opposing **Territory**;
- capture an opposing **Territory**;
- set a card as your **Gambit**;
- choose a card as your **Tactic**;
- bank a card as an **Asset**.

Avoid constructions that are grammatical only because a game term is being treated like a code token, such as:

- "win their Last Stand";
- "perform Occupation";
- "resolve Capture" when the intended meaning is simply "capture a Territory."

Named phases and procedures may still be referred to as nouns when the sentence is actually discussing that phase or procedure. For example, "During **Capture**, check whether your Front Line advances" is natural because **Capture** is the name of a turn step.

## Prefer player actions over abstract noun phrases

When both are correct, prefer the verb that describes the game action:

- **capture** rather than "perform a Capture";
- **occupy** rather than "enter Occupation" unless the state itself is the subject;
- **advance** rather than "perform Advancement";
- **set a Gambit** rather than "execute the Gambit procedure."

Technical precision does not require unnatural prose. The Comprehensive Rules may be formal, but they should still read as English rather than serialized rule identifiers.

## Prefer low cognitive density over low word count

The goal of teaching material is not to minimize the number of words or pages. It is to minimize the time and mental effort required for a player to reach confident play.

A longer explanation is preferable when its sequencing, example, whitespace, or concrete language lets the reader understand the rule on the first pass. Do not compress a concept into denser prose merely because the compressed version contains fewer words.

When editing teaching material:

- remove repetition that does not help the reader form or retain the mental model;
- preserve repetition when it deliberately reinforces a distinction the player is likely to confuse;
- introduce a distinction before the reader is likely to need it;
- keep each paragraph focused on a manageable amount of new machinery;
- prefer concrete sequences before abstract summaries;
- use a short example when it prevents the reader from having to reconstruct the rule mentally;
- use headings, whitespace, lists, diagrams, and callouts to separate concepts that should not be learned as one dense block;
- do not use an arbitrary page-count or percentage-reduction target as a substitute for comprehension testing.

For the Player's Guide, the working target is that a typical new player can read the shared-game material once, in roughly 15–20 comfortable minutes, and feel ready to begin playing without studying it.

## Use visuals to teach, not merely decorate

Visuals are part of the explanation. Prefer instructional visuals when they can show a spatial relationship, sequence, state change, card region, or contrast more quickly than prose alone.

A useful instructional visual should do at least one of these jobs:

1. anchor a concept when it is introduced;
2. reduce rereading by making a relationship or sequence visible;
3. break up a cognitively dense run of prose without separating the explanation from its context;
4. give the player a memorable at-table mental model.

Reuse an existing validated visual or renderer before designing a duplicate. When the current visual is accurate but visually dated, revise or restyle the existing source rather than inventing a competing explanation.

Decorative art is welcome when it improves pacing, faction identity, or willingness to keep reading, but it must not displace the instructional visual needed to understand the surrounding rule.

Place diagrams next to the rule they explain. A visual that requires the reader to flip to another section to understand the current paragraph has usually failed its teaching purpose.

For procedures, the graphic must preserve meaningful order. For example, a battle-sequence diagram must show that the attacker commits a Gambit before the defender and later chooses a Tactic before the defender; presenting those choices as simultaneous would teach the rule incorrectly.

## Standard callout roles

Use a small, consistent vocabulary of callouts so readers know what kind of help they are receiving:

- **Remember** — a high-value retention point.
- **Example** — a concrete application of the nearby rule.
- **Common mistake** — a likely misreading worth preventing explicitly.
- **At the table** — a procedural or component-handling reminder.
- **Why this matters** — a short explanation of an otherwise unintuitive distinction; use sparingly.

A callout should carry information that is genuinely easier to absorb outside the main paragraph. Do not move essential rules into sidebars merely to make the body appear shorter.

## Surface-specific application

- **Player's Guide:** natural player language is the default; introduce a defined term only when knowing the term helps the player read cards, references, or later rules. Optimize for first-read comprehension and use diagrams/examples aggressively where they remove conceptual reconstruction.
- **Faction Guides:** teach faction mechanics in ordinary language while preserving the exact rule meaning. Reuse faction components and state diagrams when they make resource/progression systems visible.
- **Comprehensive Rules:** use precise definitions and timing, but keep sentences grammatical and action-oriented. Diagrams may clarify difficult timing or state relationships but never substitute for the exact rule text.
- **Reference Cards:** compression may shorten grammar, but labels should still describe recognizable player actions and procedures. Visual hierarchy should support rapid lookup rather than tutorial reading.
- **Rules Arbiter:** answer in normal conversational English by default, even when grounding the ruling in formal technical rules.

## Review tests

When reviewing a sentence containing a defined term, ask:

> Would this sentence still sound natural if the term were not capitalized or visually emphasized?

If not, rewrite the sentence unless the awkwardness is necessary to distinguish the named phase, state, or technical object being discussed.

When reviewing a teaching passage, also ask:

- Can a new player understand this on the first read?
- Is each new rule connected to a mental model the reader already has?
- Would removing this sentence make the remaining prose denser or more abstract?
- Would a small diagram, example, or callout save more comprehension time than it adds reading time?
- Are paired concepts such as Position/control, Occupation/Capture, Hand/Reserve, Gambit/Tactic, and Opening/Denouement kept distinct rather than compressed together?
