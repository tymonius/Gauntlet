Diplomats · faction guide

Every battle begins with a choice.

Diplomats use Influence and Terms to turn an impending battle into a negotiation. The opponent may accept a settlement—or refuse and fight after the Diplomat has shaped the consequences.

At the table

How the Diplomats play.

Diplomats do not avoid Gauntlet’s battles; they make every battle politically expensive. A Proposal can resolve peacefully when accepted or create leverage, penalties, and ratification opportunities when refused.

Offer meaningful choices

Before cards are committed, choose an eligible Proposal, stake its Influence, and let the opponent accept or refuse.

Manage risk

Accepted Terms return the stake and may reward you. Refused Terms can be imposed by winning—but losing may cost the staked Influence.

Build legitimacy

Each newly ratified Proposal becomes a Treaty Article. Five different Articles create the Peace Treaty victory.

A good fit for you if

Choose Diplomats when...

  • You enjoy presenting opponents with difficult choices.
  • You like balancing long-term progress against short-term tactical spending.
  • You want an alternate victory that still depends on confrontation and board position.

Signature system

The mechanics that define the faction.

01

Influence

Begin with 1 Influence and build toward a maximum of 10. Influence is both political capital and a tactical battle resource.

02

Terms and Proposals

Nine reusable Proposals define requirements, stakes, accepted effects, and refused effects. Acceptance prevents the battle; refusal continues into it.

03

Leverage and ratification

After refused Terms, spend available Influence for battle bonuses. Winning can impose and ratify the Proposal even after the opponent refused it.

Victory

How the Diplomats win.

Run the Gauntlet

Diplomats can win through the normal territorial route, using settlements and political pressure to improve their position.

Peace Treaty

At the start of your turn, after Capture, five different ratified Proposals win the game. The alternate path is public, gradual, and increasingly difficult for the opponent to ignore.

Faction Leaders

Two ways to lead.

Each Leader uses the same faction card pool and core system, but pushes it toward a different tactical identity.

Agreement, card flow, and settlement

Ambassador

“Words first. War last.”

The Ambassador converts accepted Terms into additional cards, sustaining further negotiations and defenses.

Leader ability

Cordiality: once per turn, after the opponent accepts your Terms, draw one card.

Risk management, resilience, and imposition

Senator

“Procedure endures.”

The Senator can preserve staked Influence after losing a battle by permanently sacrificing cards from hand.

Leader ability

Political Capital: once per turn, sacrifice cards from hand to recover an equal amount of Influence that would otherwise be lost after refused Terms.

Archetype and setting

An institution—and a method of power.

Diplomats represent courts, envoys, assemblies, councils, and the political networks through which recognition, agreement, law, and legitimacy become forms of power. The Ambassador works through negotiation, correspondence, personal trust, and relationships across jurisdictions; the Senator works through legislation, procedure, rhetoric, coalition, and institutional authority.

Leader inspirations: Ambassador — Benjamin Franklin, Thomas Jefferson, and Talleyrand: the cultivated envoy, negotiator, and political correspondent. Senator — John Adams, Edmund Burke, and the classical tradition of Cicero: the legislator and orator who exercises power through law, argument, and procedure.

Gauntlet takes place in an eighteenth-century powder-and-parchment world of competing provinces, chartered territories, autonomous cities, commercial possessions, and independent native nations. The six factions are institutional spheres that cross political and cultural borders: methods of exercising power rather than six countries or peoples.
